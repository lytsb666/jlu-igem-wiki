Introduction

Respiratory infectious diseases have high incidence rates, spread rapidly, and readily cause cluster outbreaks, making them an important global public health issue [1]. The difficulty of early diagnosis mainly arises from the wide variety of respiratory pathogens and the highly similar clinical manifestations caused by different pathogens, making accurate differentiation based solely on nonspecific symptoms such as fever, cough, sore throat, and fatigue difficult. The challenges in transmission control and diagnosis are rooted in the diversity and high transmissibility of respiratory pathogens, as well as the highly similar early symptoms caused by different pathogens, which make accurate differentiation based solely on clinical manifestations difficult. Among them, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus subtype B (hMPV-B), Streptococcus pneumoniae (SPN), Bordetella pertussis (BP), and Haemophilus influenzae (HI) are representative respiratory infectious disease pathogens because of their broad prevalence, overlapping symptoms, high transmission risk, and potential to cause severe disease. Therefore, it is necessary to establish rapid and accurate detection methods targeting the sequences of these pathogens to improve the efficiency of early etiological diagnosis, guide timely intervention, interrupt transmission, and reduce the risk of severe disease [2].

Current methods for detecting respiratory infectious disease pathogens rely mainly on real-time quantitative PCR. Although this method is accurate and reliable, it has limitations such as complicated operation and long assay times [3]. Consequently, point-of-care detection technologies, including immunological rapid tests and isothermal amplification, have been developed both domestically and internationally. However, the former remains constrained by the antibody window period and cannot adequately meet the requirements for early detection at low pathogen loads, whereas the latter is affected by nonspecific amplification, leaving bottlenecks in detection performance. In recent years, CRISPR-associated protein 12a (CRISPR/Cas12a), owing to its efficient trans-cleavage activity, single-nucleotide-level recognition specificity, and ease of operation, has provided a new strategy for addressing these challenges and has demonstrated reliable performance superior to conventional PCR in the detection of SARS-CoV-2 and monkeypox virus [4, 5]. The wet-lab principle of RPA-CRISPR is shown in Figure 1.

However, the development of existing RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection systems still relies largely on empirical trial and error. The performance of crRNAs and RPA primers is jointly affected by multiple factors, including target sequence, secondary structure, and amplification efficiency [6], while complex parameter-coupling relationships also exist among reaction components such as Cas12a, RNP, Mg2+, and reporter molecules. As a result, the development cycle for detection systems targeting new sequences is long, experimental costs are high, and optimization outcomes lack systematicity and interpretability [7].

To overcome these technical bottlenecks, this study used respiratory syncytial virus as a model and a simulated plasmid carrying an RSV N-gene fragment to establish a multidimensional collaborative optimization system with a DBTL spiral iterative feedback mechanism. First, the DNABERT model was used to efficiently screen primer and crRNA candidates at the sequence level, followed by molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms underlying molecular binding modes and conformational changes among the components. The detection performance of the primers and crRNAs was then experimentally validated and fed back to the intelligent model, completing the first closed-loop optimization. Second, an enzyme kinetic model was introduced to accurately predict the adaptation patterns of reaction parameters and the characteristics of signal kinetic changes. Wet-lab validation was then used in reverse to calibrate model parameters and iterate the results, completing the second closed-loop optimization. Finally, we further achieved detection optimization for four respiratory pathogens. Closely following the core concept of the design-build-test-learn (DBTL) closed-loop iterative cycle, we constructed a spiral closed-loop optimization system integrating algorithmic prediction, mechanistic analysis, simulation-based optimization, and experimental validation, as shown in Figure 2.

1 Design and Synthesis of Biological Components

1.1 Biosynthesis of Cas12 Protein

1.1.1 Design of the LbCas12a Expression Construct

LbCas12a derived from Lachnospiraceae bacterium ND2006 was selected as the target protein. The corresponding coding sequence was obtained based on the defined amino acid sequence and codon-optimized for the Escherichia coli expression system. While keeping the amino acid sequence of the target protein unchanged, the coding sequence was adaptively optimized to improve recombinant expression in E. coli.

The optimized LbCas12a CDS was cloned into the pET28a-TEV expression vector. Expression of the target protein was driven by the T7 promoter, and a His-tag was introduced to enable subsequent Ni-NTA affinity purification. The resulting recombinant expression plasmid was designated pET28a-TEV-LbCas12a and, after sequence confirmation, was used for subsequent protein expression.

1.1.2 Recombinant Expression and Purification of LbCas12a

The constructed pET28a-TEV-LbCas12a recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) as the expression host. Positive clones were cultured and expanded under kanamycin-containing conditions. When the culture reached an OD600 of 0.6-0.8, IPTG induction was initiated, followed by continued culture at 16°C to obtain recombinant LbCas12a.

After induction, the cells were harvested and subjected to cell lysis and centrifugation at low temperature to obtain the soluble protein fraction containing LbCas12a. The His-tag introduced in the expression construct was used for Ni-NTA affinity purification. Host-derived nonspecific proteins were progressively removed through binding, washing, and elution, and the LbCas12a-enriched fraction was collected. The target protein was then dialyzed and concentrated by ultrafiltration to obtain recombinant LbCas12a for subsequent functional experiments.

1.1.3 Validation of the cis-Cleavage Activity of Recombinant LbCas12a

An 829-bp target dsDNA was selected as the reaction substrate, and the corresponding crRNA was designed. After LbCas12a and crRNA were assembled into a ribonucleoprotein complex, the complex recognized the target dsDNA under crRNA guidance and performed specific cleavage, theoretically generating DNA fragments of approximately 529 bp and 300 bp. After the reaction, the cleavage products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the cis-cleavage activity of recombinant LbCas12a was evaluated by comparing the band patterns of intact target dsDNA with those of the expected cleavage fragments.

The experiment was designed to include a target DNA control, a negative control lacking crRNA, a complete recombinant LbCas12a reaction group, and commercial LbCas12a #32108 as a functional positive reference.

1.1.4 Validation of the trans-Cleavage Activity of Recombinant LbCas12a

An ssDNA reporter dual-labeled with a fluorophore and a quencher was added to the reaction system. After the LbCas12a/crRNA complex recognized the target DNA, LbCas12a was activated and nonspecifically trans-cleaved the ssDNA reporter in the system, separating the fluorophore from the quencher and releasing a fluorescence signal. The target-dependent trans-cleavage activity of recombinant LbCas12a was evaluated by recording the change in fluorescence intensity over time in real time.

A trans-cleavage system was constructed using the target DNA and crRNA corresponding to those used in the cis-cleavage validation, with an ssDNA reporter added as the signal substrate. The experiment included a complete recombinant LbCas12a reaction group, a negative control lacking target DNA, and commercial LbCas12a #32108 as a functional positive reference. Fluorescence kinetics for each system were recorded by real-time fluorescence detection.

2.2.2 Construction of the Enzyme Kinetic Model and Optimization of Reaction-Component Ratios

The model describes recombinase-primer complex formation, reverse transcription, RPA amplification, and CRISPR-Cas12a-mediated reporter cleavage. DNA generated by reverse transcription enters RPA amplification, and the amplified target DNA is recognized by the Cas12a-crRNA complex, triggering reporter cleavage. The model tracks changes in the concentrations of the major reactants over 25 min and uses these data to search for optimal reaction conditions within the specified ranges. 2.2.3 Systematic Optimization of the RPA-CRISPR Cascade Reaction System

a) Experimental Design

In addition to the reaction parameters described above, the detection performance of the RPA-CRISPR cascade reaction system is jointly affected by reaction temperature, final reporter concentration, RNP ratio (crRNA:Cas12a), and Mg2+ concentration. Therefore, we further used the RPA-CRISPR cascade amplification detection system described in Section 2.1 and applied a single-factor optimization method to optimize these key reaction parameters individually. The reaction temperatures were set to 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, and 42°C; the final reporter concentrations were set to 500, 750, 1000, 1500, and 2000 nM; the RNP ratios were set to 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3; and the Mg2+ concentrations were set to 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 mM. The difference in signal between positive samples and negative controls in the fluorescence amplification curves under different conditions was used as the evaluation criterion to identify the reaction conditions with the best detection performance. The reaction was performed at 40°C, with the instrument set to 50 cycles, and fluorescence signals were collected in real time. Detailed system parameters are shown in Table 3.

b) Experimental Results
i) Temperature:

As shown in Figure 11, the optimal reaction temperature for the RPA-CRISPR cascade reaction system was 41°C. This indicates that 41°C is an appropriate temperature for coordinated RPA amplification and Cas12a cleavage, enabling optimal fluorescence signal output.

ii) Mg2+ Concentration:

As shown in Figure 12, the optimal Mg2+ concentration for the RPA-CRISPR cascade reaction system was 25 mM. As a key enzyme cofactor, Mg2+ at 25 mM can balance amplification and cleavage activities, whereas deviations from this concentration inhibit overall reaction performance.

iii) Probe Concentration:

As shown in Figure 13, the optimal probe concentration for the RPA-CRISPR cascade reaction system was 2000 nM. This indicates that at 2000 nM, the probe concentration provides sufficient cleavage substrate to generate a high signal while avoiding increased background caused by excessive probe.

iv) RNP Ratio (gRNA:Cas12a):

As shown in Figure 14, the optimal RNP ratio for the RPA-CRISPR cascade reaction system was 1:3. This result indicates that, relative to gRNA, an appropriately higher proportion of Cas12a facilitates sufficient assembly of functional RNP complexes and enhances nuclease cleavage efficiency after target activation.

2.2.4 Iteration and Bias Correction of the Dry-Lab Model Based on Wet-Lab Validation

Based on the experimental validation of the candidate primer-crRNA sequence screening results and optimized parameters described above, we fed the wet-lab data back into the simulation model and continuously corrected discrepancies between model predictions and experimental results through parameter updates and iterative calibration, thereby forming a closed-loop iterative workflow of 'model prediction - experimental validation - data feedback - model optimization.' Through this continuous cycle, we progressively improved the predictive accuracy, stability, and reliability of the model for the RPA-CRISPR reaction system and provided more accurate theoretical guidance for subsequent experimental design and parameter optimization.

We selected simulated plasmids carrying conserved genes from three non-target viruses (human metapneumovirus subtype B, SARS-CoV-2, and monkeypox virus) for specificity validation to evaluate the recognition specificity of the detection system. The criterion for specificity was that the detection system should generate a significant positive fluorescence signal only for the target simulated plasmid, while all other non-target simulated plasmids should yield negative results. All specificity validation tests were performed using the fully parameter-optimized RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection workflow. The reaction was performed at 40°C, with the instrument set to 50 cycles, and fluorescence signals were collected in real time. Detailed system parameters are shown in Table 9.-
3.2.2 Experimental Results

The specificity validation results showed that the complete RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system generated a positive fluorescence signal only for the target simulated plasmid. No obvious fluorescence increase was observed for any of the tested non-target simulated plasmids, and their signal levels remained essentially consistent with those of the negative-control group. These results indicate that the parameter-optimized detection system has excellent recognition specificity, can effectively distinguish the target pathogen from other non-target pathogens, and meets the specificity requirements for actual sample testing. The results are shown in Figure 16.-

3.3 Reproducibility Evaluation

3.3.1 Experimental Design

Detection reproducibility was evaluated through six parallel experiments performed by two operators at different time points over two days. Reproducibility was judged based on whether the variation in fluorescence signals across repeated experiments remained within a reasonable range. Reproducibility was evaluated using the optimized experimental protocol of the combined RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system. The reaction was performed at 40°C, with the instrument set to 50 cycles, and fluorescence signals were collected in real time. Detailed system parameters are shown in Table 9.-

3.3.2 Experimental Results

Two experimenters conducted parallel experiments at different time points. The individual plots are shown in Figure 17, presenting six independent fluorescence kinetic curves obtained by the two operators on different dates and at different times. The upward trends of the curves for the positive samples were generally consistent, with only slight fluctuations among the different experimental groups, whereas the negative-control groups remained at baseline throughout, with no obvious increase in fluorescence signal. All six positive experimental datasets were then integrated to generate the overlaid curve plot shown in Figure 18. The high degree of overlap among the multiple positive kinetic curves provides a direct indication of system stability. The fluorescence signals at the 25-min reaction endpoint were further obtained for all six experiments and averaged, yielding a mean of 29688.90. The standard deviation of these six data points was then calculated as 1534.98. Finally, dividing the standard deviation by the mean gave a coefficient of variation (CV) of approximately 5.17%.

Together, the visual performance of the individual and overlaid plots and the quantitative CV calculation demonstrate that the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system has good inter-operator and inter-batch reproducibility.

3.4 Interference-Resistance Evaluation

3.4.1 Experimental Design

To simulate the testing scenario of actual clinical throat-swab samples, we evaluated matrix interference resistance. Actual throat-swab samples contain salivary components, and lysis buffer must also be added during sample pretreatment to lyse the pathogen. These matrix components may interfere with the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a reaction, making it necessary to validate detection-system performance under saliva-lysis-buffer conditions. In this study, a mixed saliva-lysis-buffer-water system was used to dilute the target plasmid and simulate actual throat-swab testing conditions. Plasmid concentrations close to the limit of detection were selected to establish dilution gradients, and interference resistance was assessed based on whether the system could stably distinguish positive samples from negative controls in this complex matrix environment. The interference-resistance evaluation was performed using the fully optimized combined RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system, with detailed reaction parameters shown in Table 9.----

3.4.2 Experimental Results

The interference-resistance evaluation showed that the overall trends of the fluorescence growth curves were similar between the saliva-lysis-buffer matrix group and the pure-water control group, with only small differences in the fluorescence signals of the positive samples; the positive signal in the saliva-containing group was slightly lower than that in the pure-water control group. Even in the presence of the complex saliva-lysis-buffer matrix, the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system could still stably identify the target simulated plasmid and clearly distinguish positive samples from negative controls. The negative controls in all groups maintained stable baselines, with no nonspecific fluorescence increase. These results confirm that the optimized detection system has good resistance to matrix interference. Complex components in saliva and the lysis buffer exerted only slight inhibition on the reaction and did not significantly interfere with detection performance, indicating good applicability for subsequent testing of actual clinical saliva samples. The results are shown in Figure 19.-

9 Application Potential

Our project established an integrated measurement-optimization paradigm combining DNABERT-based intelligent sequence screening, molecular-dynamics interaction simulation, enzyme kinetic modeling, and DBTL wet-lab closed-loop calibration, thereby providing a standardized development workflow for RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a fluorescence-based quantitative measurement systems. By using dry-lab simulation for preliminary screening, we can analyze in advance the binding conformations, binding affinities, and dynamic interaction patterns between crRNA and Cas12a protein and eliminate candidate sequences with poor compatibility or potential off-target risks. This replaces extensive repetitive blind screening in the wet lab, effectively shortening the overall development process for primer screening and reaction-condition adjustment, reducing development costs associated with consumable use and manual trial and error, and accelerating iterative refinement of the detection system. This measurement-optimization framework has excellent transferability and is not limited to the respiratory pathogens used in the initial study, such as respiratory syncytial virus. By replacing the conserved target-gene sequence, crRNA screening and preliminary reaction-parameter prediction can be rapidly performed for influenza viruses, adenoviruses, gastrointestinal viruses, vector-borne pathogens, and emerging variant strains. The framework can therefore serve as a general development paradigm for constructing rapid detection schemes for a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms.-

The complete isothermal fluorescence-based quantitative measurement system, combined with our self-developed portable fluorescence measurement device, can be deployed for diverse practical testing needs. It can enable accurate identification of low-copy samples during the early stage of infection, support disease-control personnel in conducting on-site screening at locations of cluster outbreaks, and shorten response times for emerging infectious diseases. It can also provide routine pathogen-load monitoring for susceptible populations such as children and older adults. The lyophilized prefabricated reaction components can be developed into home self-testing kits to meet household health-screening needs. The optimization workflow can also be extended to animal husbandry for on-site detection of zoonotic pathogens originating from livestock and poultry. Our approach establishes a complete chain linking virtual simulation optimization, wet-lab closed-loop iteration, portable hardware support, and multi-scenario practical deployment. It addresses limitations of conventional nucleic acid detection, including long research-and-development cycles, high trial-and-error costs, and the unsuitability of large instruments for field measurements. It provides a reusable standardized technical paradigm for fluorescence-based quantitative measurement of various pathogenic microorganisms and has multiple practical applications in clinical pathogen diagnosis, emergency public health response, routine public health monitoring, and prevention and control of livestock and poultry diseases.
