Overview

This project is based on a synthetic-biology nucleic acid detection strategy. RPA isothermal amplification is used to rapidly enrich pathogen targets, while the CRISPR-Cas12a cleavage-based fluorescent reporter system provides specific recognition and signal output, forming an integrated single-tube detection platform for simultaneous screening of five viruses. In vitro nucleic acid amplification carries safety risks such as aerosol contamination and accidental plasmid release. Throughout the project, no live pathogenic bacteria or engineered live microorganisms are used; only artificially inactivated plasmids are used as simulated templates, reducing biosafety risks at the source. From the experimental-design stage onward, we established a full-chain safety scheme covering single-tube closed reactions, laboratory control, and waste disinfection. We strictly follow domestic biosafety regulations and iGEM guidelines, proactively identifying and mitigating potential risks to protect both personnel and the environment.

Lab Safety

3.1 Laboratory Information

The experiments were conducted in a Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory at the School of Public Health, Jilin University. The laboratory complies with the General Guidelines for Biosafety in Pathogenic Microorganism Laboratories of the People's Republic of China as well as relevant laboratory-safety regulations for higher-education institutions. This project involves only in vitro nucleic acid experiments and does not culture live pathogens or engineered live microorganisms.

3.2 Safe and Proper Use of Equipment

01Biosafety cabinet, used for reagent aliquoting, reaction-system preparation, and plasmid loading to limit the spread of nucleic acid aerosols;

02Clean bench, used to provide a sterile, highly clean working environment;

03Autoclave, used to sterilize nucleic-acid-contaminated consumables and liquid waste;

04Centrifuge, vortex mixer, and PCR instrument, used for RPA and CRISPR fluorescence detection;

05Personal protective equipment such as safety goggles, nitrile gloves, and laboratory protective clothing, worn properly throughout experiments;

06Supporting emergency facilities such as emergency showers and eyewash stations, firefighting equipment, first-aid kits, and emergency lighting;

07Entry and exit registration.

3.3 Personal Operational Safety

3.3 .1 Safety Training

Before conducting experiments, all wet-lab team members must complete systematic safety-access training covering basic biosafety knowledge, BSL-2 laboratory operating rules, personal protective practices, environmental disinfection procedures, instrument-use requirements, and emergency-response procedures for various leakage incidents. The team also receives dedicated training on RPA-CRISPR operating procedures, nucleic acid aerosol prevention and control, plasmid management, and disposal of contaminated waste.

3.3 .2 Safety Requirements

Before entering the laboratory, personnel must properly wear a lab coat, N95 mask, double-layer nitrile gloves, and safety goggles. Eating and drinking are strictly prohibited in the experimental area, and contact between reagents and the face must be avoided. All experimental operations are fully recorded in the laboratory notebook, with parallel records retained for disinfection, reagent use, and workstation use.

3.4 Waste Disposal

Laboratory waste is strictly sorted, collected, and disposed of according to classification.

01Solid biological waste: consumables contaminated with plasmids, nucleic acids, or reaction mixtures, including pipette tips, eight-tube strips, and centrifuge tubes, are collected in yellow biohazard bags, autoclaved, and then transferred to a qualified organization for centralized collection and disposal. Sharps are placed directly into puncture-resistant sharps containers.

02Liquid waste: nucleic-acid-containing waste and residual reaction mixtures are thoroughly soaked in sodium hypochlorite to degrade nucleic acids. Chemical waste that does not contain biological materials is sorted into dedicated waste containers for centralized collection and disposal.

03General household waste is packaged separately and must never be mixed with biologically contaminated waste.

Design Safety

Traditional stepwise RPA-CRISPR detection requires opening the tube after amplification to add downstream reaction components, which can readily release nucleic acid aerosols and cause cross-contamination in the laboratory.

After multiple rounds of gradient optimization, we established a layered, sealed, single-tube reaction system: RPA premix, plasmid template, MgSO4 solution and the CRISPR-RNP complex are mixed at the bottom of the tube, with all components initially sealed in the same centrifuge tube. The reaction remains sealed throughout, with no need for a second lid-opening or pipetting step.

This molecular-level design eliminates the need to open tubes after amplification, substantially reducing the risk of nucleic acid aerosol generation and forming a two-layer safety barrier together with biosafety-cabinet operating procedures.

Biosafety

5.1 Plasmid Application Safety

5.1 .1 Purpose

Conventional respiratory-pathogen experiments require handling live microorganisms, creating risks of infection and biological spread. To avoid these hazards at the source, we plan to use artificial plasmids as simulated pathogen templates and construct a chassis-free system without introducing live pathogenic bacteria or live viruses at any stage.

5.1 .2 Design

We artificially constructed recombinant plasmids carrying specific conserved sequences from five respiratory pathogens to replace live pathogens for performance validation of the RPA-CRISPR system. The plasmids can support nucleic acid amplification only in vitro, have no autonomous infectivity, and cannot persistently replicate in the natural environment. Every laboratory use and transfer is fully registered to strictly control the risk of accidental plasmid release.

5.1 .3 Conclusion

By using plasmids to simulate pathogens, this project eliminates the need to handle live pathogens and establishes a live-microorganism-free experimental system, greatly reducing the risks of biological infection and environmental colonization. Standardized storage and access procedures further ensure that plasmid materials remain under control.

5.2 Genetic Element Safety

5.2 .1 Purpose

The CRISPR-Cas12a system has nucleic acid cleavage activity, so the full set of genetic elements must be assessed for off-target effects and potential biological toxicity to ensure the safe use of these molecular components.

5.2 .2 Design

The primers and crRNAs used in this project were screened by machine learning and specifically recognize only the target sequences of five respiratory pathogens. Sequence alignment confirmed that the target regions have no significant homology with the human genome, indicating no risk of off-target cleavage of the human genome. The complete set of detection elements performs only target recognition and fluorescent signal output and does not express pathogenic factors or toxin proteins. All nucleic acid operations are conducted in a biosafety cabinet, and the single-tube closed system further reduces amplicon dispersal.

5.2 .3 Conclusion

Following sequence design and safety assessment, the genetic elements used in this project show high specificity, no evident off-target risk, and no inherent biological toxicity. The combination of molecular-level and operational safeguards effectively controls risks associated with these genetic elements.

6.1 Compliance with Policies and Regulations

All team activities strictly comply with international iGEM competition rules, official institutional policies, and national laws and regulations, ensuring that the project is conducted within a legal and compliant framework.

6.1 .1 iGEM Safety Policy

We fully understand iGEM's core requirements for safety and responsibility and regard them as a foundation for project success. Accordingly, we have taken the following measures:

Biological material selection: our first priority is to select biological materials included on the iGEM White List. Any material not included on the White List is subject to strict verification and risk assessment before experimentation.

Risk-group assessment: we fully understand the four biosafety risk-group classifications and formally commit not to use any organism, or any part of an organism, belonging to Risk Group 3 or Risk Group 4, thereby minimizing potential risks to the greatest extent possible.

Biocontainment and waste disposal: we ensure that no biological material is released at any stage of the experiments. All experimental waste is properly handled by the laboratory supervisor in accordance with strict procedures to prevent harm to the environment or personnel.

Human testing principles: we strictly comply with iGEM's policy prohibiting human experimentation. To date, no product testing has been conducted on humans, and all research has been performed in strictly controlled laboratory environments. Any work involving human samples must receive prior approval from the iGEM Safety and Security Committee.

Human-subject research: all surveys, interviews, and public-engagement activities are conducted in accordance with applicable laws, regulations, and institutional guidelines.

6.1 .2 Official Institutional Policies

All experimental activities conducted by our team strictly follow Jilin University's laboratory-safety management regulations. All students and researchers involved in experiments must complete the following training and requirements:

Mandatory safety training: all students taking laboratory courses must complete a compulsory laboratory-safety course during their first semester and pass the examination before entering the laboratory to conduct experiments. The course covers classification of hazardous reagents, risks associated with commonly used chemicals, fire-safety measures, personal protective measures, standard experimental procedures, laboratory rules, and related topics.

Operating procedures: during experiments, we strictly comply with laboratory-safety rules and the School's laboratory-safety management procedures to ensure biosafety and the safety of all personnel.

6.1 .3 Compliance with National Regulations

Our experiments strictly comply with the national Measures for the Safety Administration of Genetic Engineering. We reviewed the specific provisions of these regulations to ensure that every stage of the project meets national standards:

Safety-level assessment: according to Articles 6 and 8 of the Measures for the Safety Administration of Genetic Engineering, our project is classified as Safety Level 1, meaning that it poses no risk to human health or the environment.

Vector and host assessment: in accordance with Article 9, after in-depth discussions with our advisors, we identified the vectors and hosts used in the project and confirmed that our researchers and laboratory possess the capabilities and conditions required to mitigate potential hazards.

Project review and approval: in accordance with Articles 13, 14, and 15, our project was submitted to the School for review and received formal approval from the School's administrative department, ensuring project compliance.

Laboratory management and emergency response: in accordance with Articles 19, 20, and 21, we strictly follow laboratory-safety regulations. Laboratory waste is handled through cooperation between the laboratory supervisor and a professional biochemical-waste disposal organization. All researchers have completed comprehensive emergency-response training and mastered standard experimental techniques for responding to possible emergencies.

Records and supervision: in accordance with Article 24, detailed records of laboratory use and safety supervision are maintained in logs and securely retained for inspection.

6.2 Safety, Ethics, and Data Compliance Design—Building a “Foundation of Responsibility” for Point-of-Care Detection Technology

As the technical plan has become increasingly clear, we have recognized that for a point-of-care detection technology intended to serve public health, especially older adults and children, safety and ethics are not a static “checklist” but a “lifeline” running throughout the entire project. We strictly follow international ethical requirements and embed the concept of “responsible innovation” into the project's DNA. More importantly, we established a dynamic feedback mechanism that grows together with our stakeholders, ensuring that our technology is not only reliable in the laboratory but also trusted and accepted in real community settings. At the core of this mechanism is continuous dialogue with experts, policymakers, and our target users, especially older adults and children, so that their voices are incorporated into every stage from the very beginning of the project.

To ensure that technological innovation always proceeds along a responsible development pathway, the team follows the core strategy of “working closely with regulatory authorities and policymakers, conducting systematic policy research, establishing a coordinated ‘R&D-compliance-policy’ feedback channel, regularly consulting cross-disciplinary experts, and sharing real-world data to provide practical evidence for policy optimization.” Based on this strategy, we built a comprehensive “responsibility framework” for the rapid-detection technology platform across four dimensions: biosafety, data privacy, ethical equity, and regulatory foresight.

6.2 .1 Biosafety Design: Ensuring Environmental and Operator Safety

Portable detection devices may be used in diverse settings including homes, primary-care sites, and ports of entry, making biosafety risk control in complex environments a top priority. Based on our core collaboration strategy, we contacted Zhao Wei, Director of the Institute of Microbiology at the Jilin Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, for an in-depth discussion. Incorporating the professional recommendation that “live pathogens and active enzyme preparations require dual physical and chemical containment,” we established a multidimensional safety-protection system:

Closed-tube detection design: an innovative closed-tube detection design allows the entire process of nucleic acid amplification and CRISPR target recognition to be completed inside a sealed microfluidic chip, preventing aerosol leakage at the source.

Risk control for core components: inactivated nucleases or strictly controllable enzyme preparations are prioritized to reduce the biosafety risks associated with core components.

Built-in device disinfection: the device includes an integrated UV disinfection module that automatically disinfects the surface of the chip compartment after testing.

Chip sealing and waste disposal: in response to expert requirements for “verification of chip-sealing reliability” and “standardized waste disposal,” we further strengthened the chip-sealing performance test procedure and designed a dedicated disposable collection bag for used chips, while reserving a technical interface for future integration of in situ inactivation functions.

Application-scenario risk assessment: we conducted an in-depth risk assessment of practical use scenarios. In addition to evaluating conventional risks of pathogen leakage, we paid particular attention to secondary risks caused by misuse of the device by non-professionals, such as caregivers of older adults or curious children, in home or community settings. We therefore simulated scenarios such as accidental device drops and children touching the device, and used the results to strengthen the physical locking design of the chip compartment and the impact resistance of the device housing.

User-feedback-driven improvement: after the initial design, we consulted the head nurse of a community hospital. She commented, “The dedicated collection bag you designed is very good, but many older people may not remember complicated waste-sorting rules. Could the bag itself have a very prominent label, for example a large ‘biohazard’ symbol in bright yellow?” We immediately adopted this valuable suggestion and redesigned the visual system of the collection bag.

Community education on waste disposal: we plan to work with communities to develop easy-to-understand educational videos and illustrated guides on waste disposal for older adults and families, using a storytelling approach to communicate safety knowledge.

6.2 .2 Data Security and Privacy Protection Design: Addressing Personal-Information Security Challenges

The detection device exchanges data through a smart App, and test results are closely linked to personal identity and health information. Data security and privacy protection are therefore key prerequisites for real-world implementation. To better protect users' privacy, we collaborated with experts in data privacy, strictly followed the Personal Information Protection Law and relevant regulations on medical and health data, and established a full-process data-security system:

Data anonymization: when users first log in to the App, a random unique identifier (UUID) is automatically generated. All test data are linked to the UUID rather than directly to sensitive information such as national identification numbers or mobile-phone numbers.

Multi-level access control: multi-level permission-control mechanisms are applied to data during local storage on the device and transmission to the cloud.

Expert guidance and commitment: Academician Xing-Fang Li, Professor in the Department of Laboratory Medicine and Pathology at the Faculty of Medicine and Dentistry, University of Alberta, Canada, emphasized: “Data are not merely cold zeros and ones; behind them are families' hopes for health. Protecting these data, especially those of older adults and children, means protecting the future of our society. Your project must ensure that technology carries warmth.” These words deeply influenced our team and led us to regard privacy protection as a solemn commitment.

Protection of user data rights: in response to the expert recommendation to “clearly inform users of their data rights, obtain explicit consent, and provide a pathway for data deletion,” we comprehensively improved the App's privacy-policy text and deeply integrated the “informed consent” process into the user interface. We also provide convenient channels for users to close their accounts and delete historical data, making privacy protection visible, tangible, and accessible.

Ethical protection of data for vulnerable groups: we pay particular attention to “protection of vulnerable groups” in data ethics. Through questionnaires, we investigated older users' and their families' understanding of and concerns about health-data sharing. We then developed targeted privacy designs. For older adults, in addition to privacy settings within the App, we designed a physical “privacy card.” Family members can help older adults indicate on the card whether they are willing to anonymously contribute data for public-health research. During testing, scanning the QR code on the card allows preferences to be set quickly, helping to reduce the digital divide.

6.2 .3 Ethical and Social-Equity Considerations: Ensuring Accessibility and Fairness

We are keenly aware that the introduction of new technologies may create a “technology divide.” Following the collaborative requirements of our core strategy, we held a focused discussion with a public-health policy researcher, Yan Yifeng of the Medical Administration Division of the Jilin Provincial Health Commission, and incorporated accessibility and fairness into the fundamental logic of the technology design. Based on Yan's recommendation to “plan pathways for technology deployment to primary-level settings in advance, while taking both price and public education into account,” we implemented targeted optimizations during device development:

Power accessibility: improved circuit design reduces device power consumption and supports power supply from solar power banks, helping overcome electricity limitations in resource-constrained areas.

Universal result interpretation: in addition to detailed display through the smart App, we retained intuitive LED indicator lights and added voice announcements (“Testing complete. The result is negative.”) to meet the basic interpretation needs of older adults and residents in remote areas. For children, we worked with primary-school teachers to design a friendly cartoon health ambassador for the App's result interface and used positive language such as “Your immune-system warriors defeated the virus monster!” to explain results and reduce children's anxiety.

Cost and public education: in response to experts' concern that “new technologies may have relatively high initial costs,” we formally incorporated science communication and social outreach into the long-term Human Practices plan. In the future, we will explore approaches such as collaboration with public-health programs and development of product versions with different configurations so that the benefits of the technology can truly reach every group.

User-feedback-driven iteration: we initially believed that LED indicator lights were already intuitive enough. However, during a preliminary community survey, an older participant commented, “The lights keep flashing, and I can't remember which color is the good one.” We immediately realized that “intuitive” information needed to be simplified further. We therefore added a simple voice-announcement function to the device and redesigned the user interface, completing another key iteration.

6.2 .4 Regulatory Foresight: Aligning with the Future Product-Development Pathway

Based on the core strategy of coordinated “R&D-compliance-policy” feedback, we initiated systematic research into regulatory requirements for in vitro diagnostic (IVD) devices. Through consultation with Yan Yifeng of the Medical Administration Division of the Jilin Provincial Health Commission, we clarified the key requirement that commercialization of the product would require a medical-device registration certificate from the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), and accordingly developed a forward-looking compliance plan:

Early data accumulation: from the early stages of the project, we simultaneously accumulated key methodological validation data, including sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability, and established design-and-development documentation that meets quality-management-system requirements.

Ethics and Safety Review Committee: following Yan Yifeng's suggestion, we established an internal “Ethics and Safety Review Committee” and invited biosafety experts, public-health ethicists, and even community representatives to serve as advisors. At key milestones, they conduct a “pre-review” of our plans to ensure that the project remains on a responsible path. At the same time, Li Hui oversees and verifies the project's overall ethical considerations.

Regulators as partners: during our discussion with health-commission expert Yan Yifeng, he not only explained regulatory provisions but also emphasized: “It is excellent that young people like you are enthusiastic about innovation, but that enthusiasm should be directed toward solving real-world problems. Compliance is not a constraint; it is the safeguard that allows good technologies to truly benefit the public. We look forward to working with teams like yours to explore a regulatory-support pathway suited to the development of new technologies.” This made us realize that regulators are “partners” on our path of innovation rather than “referees.”

Through multidimensional design covering safety, ethics, and compliance, we established a comprehensive “responsibility framework” for the rapid-detection technology platform. This not only minimizes the technology's potential risks to the greatest extent possible but also ensures that its development pathway fully meets the dual expectations of laws and regulations and social ethics, thereby putting the team's established collaboration-and-compliance strategy into practice.

6.3 HP/Education Safety

To support the Human Practices (HP) and Education (Edu) activities of this project, we established a safety-management system covering the entire activity process, ensuring that every interaction takes scientific rigor, safety, and humanistic care into account.

6.3.1 Activity Safety

To avoid safety risks during HP/Edu activities, the team established a dual safety-management mechanism: “Safety Notification-Process Control.” Before any activity begins, participants, including community residents, patients, and students, receive a “Project Activity Safety Responsibility Notice” explaining the purpose, procedures, and safety precautions of the activity, while team members provide guidance throughout the entire process.

Safety-responsibility notification: for activities involving sensitive groups, such as patient interviews, we place particular emphasis on participants' psychological safety in addition to physical safety. Interviewers are trained in “empathetic communication” in advance to avoid potentially distressing or traumatic topics, and the notice clearly states that “participants have the right to pause or terminate the interview at any time,” ensuring that activity safety covers both physical and psychological dimensions.

Protection of special groups: before conducting HP activities involving older adults and children, we sign letters of intent and safety-assurance documents in advance. When conducting HP activities with people with disabilities or special-education institutions, we establish dedicated ethical-protection measures to ensure that their rights and interests are fully respected.

Safe instrument use: for device operation, we developed safety protections and user instructions specifically for vulnerable groups to ensure their safety throughout the operating process.

6.3.2 Personal Privacy Safety

Considering that HP activities may involve the collection of personal information, the team established strict privacy-safety management procedures:

Hospital activities and patient privacy: when conducting activities in hospitals or interviewing patients, we strictly follow hospital requirements and take measures to protect patient privacy.

Ethics review and approval: before conducting patient interviews, we submitted an ethics-review application to the relevant hospital and obtained a “Human Research Ethics Approval” document confirming that the interview process and scope of information use comply with medical privacy-protection regulations.

Informed consent: when collecting patients' basic information and interview content, all interviewed patients sign an “Informed Consent Form.” We clearly explain the purpose of the records and state that all information obtained will be used only for project development and analysis and will not be disclosed to any third party, thereby ensuring strict privacy protection throughout the entire process of information collection and use.

6.3.3 Ethical Safety

We actively interviewed experts in ethics, philosophy, social sciences, law, and medicine to understand the ethical issues involved in the project, obtain professional advice, develop risk-response strategies, and avoid insufficient adaptability caused by reliance on a single ethical standard. The main iHP Wiki content is centered on humanistic care, and through our device we aim to ensure that everyone can enjoy the right to prevent pathogen infection and protect respiratory health.

6.3.4 Social Responsibility

When determining the output signal of the detection system, we have always followed one core principle: the ultimate beneficiary of the technology is people, not the device itself. Therefore, we chose a simple output approach that is non-invasive, safe, and suitable for home use, especially for older adults and children in home or community settings. However, technological accessibility does not automatically mean social acceptability. Throughout the project, we repeatedly asked ourselves: how can we ensure that the device is used correctly by non-professionals, especially older adults and caregivers? How can we avoid secondary harm to vulnerable groups caused by improper operation or data leakage? How can we help primary-level communities genuinely trust and accept this new technology? These questions drove us to carry out systematic design across four dimensions—biosafety, data privacy, social equity, and regulatory compliance—and to integrate the concept of “responsible innovation” into every technical decision, ensuring that our detection system is not only reliable in the laboratory but also warm, safe, and trusted in the real world.
