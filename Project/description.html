1. Background and Challenges

Respiratory infectious diseases are characterized by high incidence, rapid transmission, and a propensity to cause cluster outbreaks, making them a major global public health concern. Early clinical manifestations caused by different pathogens are highly similar and commonly include nonspecific symptoms such as fever, cough, sore throat, and fatigue, which makes accurate differentiation based on symptoms alone difficult. Without timely diagnosis and intervention, some patients may progress to pneumonia, bronchitis, or even respiratory failure, while sustained transmission may occur within households, schools, childcare facilities, eldercare institutions, and healthcare settings. The Global Burden of Disease study indicates that lower respiratory infections excluding COVID-19 remain important causes of morbidity and mortality, with particularly substantial disease burdens among young children, older adults, and immunocompromised populations [1]. Therefore, establishing rapid and accurate methods for early pathogen detection that can be used in primary-level settings is important for improving diagnostic and treatment efficiency, supporting interruption of transmission chains, and reducing the risk of severe disease.

This project focuses on five pathogens: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus (hMPV), Streptococcus pneumoniae (SPN), Bordetella pertussis (BP), and Haemophilus influenzae (HI). Together, they encompass both viral and bacterial pathogens and are associated with a high disease burden, overlapping symptoms, occult transmission, or the risk of invasive infection. They therefore represent distinct public health concerns that require priority identification in childcare facilities, eldercare institutions, and primary healthcare settings.

Developing a rapid combined detection system targeting their conserved sequences can address the diagnostic challenge in primary-level settings where similar symptoms may require different management pathways, while also establishing a reusable technical framework for future expansion to other respiratory pathogens.

2. Limitations of Existing Detection Technologies and POCT Products

The practical need in respiratory pathogen testing is not simply to achieve a lower limit of detection. Rather, rapid, specific, stable, and interpretable results must still be obtained under conditions involving low pathogen loads during early infection, variable sample quality, limited personnel and equipment, and overlapping symptoms caused by multiple pathogens.

2.1 Limitations of existing detection technologies

Current methods for respiratory pathogen detection can be broadly divided into precise laboratory testing and rapid on-site testing. qPCR and RT-qPCR provide high sensitivity and specificity and can support quantitative or semi-quantitative analysis. However, they generally require a standardized laboratory environment, nucleic acid extraction equipment, real-time fluorescence amplification instruments, and trained personnel. Sample pretreatment, nucleic acid extraction, reaction setup, amplification, and analysis form a lengthy workflow, making these methods difficult to transfer directly to activity rooms in eldercare institutions, health rooms in kindergartens, or sites of sudden cluster outbreaks. Conventional culture, serology, and sequencing are also limited by turnaround time, diagnostic windows, complexity, or cost, respectively.

Antigen or lateral-flow assays are simple to operate and provide rapid results, but they may yield false-negative results during early infection or in samples with low pathogen loads. Antibody testing is additionally constrained by the immune-response window. Isothermal amplification reduces dependence on thermal cyclers; however, primer dimers, target-sequence secondary structures, sample impurities, and nonspecific amplification may still elevate background signals or cause false-positive results. Recent studies of multiplex respiratory POCT further demonstrate the value of combined multi-pathogen detection, but successful on-site deployment also requires systematic solutions for sample pretreatment, channel isolation, temperature control, signal readout, and user operation [8].

2.2 Limitations of current POCT products

A further gap remains between a technically feasible method and a usable POCT product. Many existing products or prototypes cover only a single pathogen or a small number of pathogens, making them poorly suited to settings where multiple pathogens co-circulate and symptoms overlap. Expanding the detection panel can introduce primer competition, channel crosstalk, and greater complexity in result interpretation. Some approaches still require off-device nucleic acid extraction, open-tube transfer, or repeated pipetting, which increase training requirements and operational errors as well as the risk of aerosol contamination by amplification products. Primary-level use also requires stable reagent storage, internal and external quality control, positive and negative controls, invalid-result prompts, device calibration, and traceable data records. These product-development elements are often overlooked during the proof-of-concept stage. Finally, a rapid result is not equivalent to a clinical diagnosis: the timing of sample collection, differentiation between colonization and infection, management following a false-negative result, and pathways for confirming positive results must all be clearly defined in the product instructions and operating procedures.

An ideal POCT for respiratory pathogens should therefore meet five interrelated requirements: low dependence on specialized settings and trained operators, a short turnaround time, stable detection at low pathogen loads, highly specific discrimination among multiple targets, and closed-loop quality control from sample processing to result recording. The technical and device designs of this project are centered on these requirements.

3. Integrated RT-RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a Solution

To address the technical challenges and product limitations described above, we first focused on recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), an efficient isothermal amplification technique characterized by a moderate reaction temperature, a relatively short amplification time, and low equipment dependence, making it well suited to primary-level screening and on-site testing. However, the amplification efficiency and specificity of an RPA system are highly dependent on primer sequences, the local structure of the target sequence, and reaction conditions. Poor primer design or complex target-sequence secondary structures may reduce amplification efficiency and lead to background amplification and the accumulation of nonspecific products, making it difficult for RPA alone to achieve both high sensitivity and high specificity.

The CRISPR/Cas12a system provides a second layer of sequence recognition to address these limitations. Guided by a crRNA, LbCas12a specifically recognizes the target double-stranded DNA and subsequently activates trans-cleavage activity, cleaving a single-stranded DNA reporter to generate a fluorescent or visual signal. Placing this step after RPA amplification can reduce the risk of false-positive results caused by nonspecific amplification products. Recent studies have demonstrated that combining RPA with CRISPR/Cas12a enables the detection of various respiratory pathogens with relatively low equipment dependence, while closed reactions and simplified sample pretreatment may further enhance its potential for on-site use [9,10].

On this basis, our project is designed for kindergartens and eldercare institutions. It integrates RT-RPA with LbCas12a trans-cleavage for multi-pathogen detection and includes the parallel development of a portable fluorescence-reading prototype. Rather than simply combining two technologies, the system uses RPA for rapid target enrichment and Cas12a for sequence-specific verification, while independent reaction channels and digital result recording reduce the complexity of multi-target interpretation.

3.1 Target pretreatment and RT-RPA amplification

Specific primers are designed against conserved gene fragments of the five pathogens. Viral RNA is reverse-transcribed and enters the RPA workflow together with bacterial DNA for isothermal amplification at approximately 37-43 °C, thereby reducing dependence on precision thermal-cycling equipment.

3.2 Specific recognition by the LbCas12a RNP complex

A target-specific crRNA forms a ribonucleoprotein complex with LbCas12a. When an amplification product matches the corresponding crRNA, Cas12a is activated and cleaves a fluorophore-quencher ssDNA reporter, converting sequence recognition into a measurable signal.

3.3 Multi-target interpretation in independent channels

Amplification and Cas12a recognition for the five pathogens are performed in separate reaction channels to reduce primer competition and signal crosstalk. The portable reading module collects fluorescence from each channel and reports positive, negative, or invalid results according to predefined thresholds.

3.4 Digital result management

A mobile interface is used to display results, store testing times and sample identifiers, and support authorized data export, thereby helping institutions establish traceable records.

4. A Multidimensional Optimization Loop Integrating Dry-Lab and Wet-Lab Work

The development of RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assays often depends on wet-lab experience and repeated trial and error. Primer and crRNA performance is jointly affected by PAM context, primer placement, base composition, sequence conservation, local secondary structure, binding efficiency, and off-target risk. At the reaction level, Cas12a, crRNA, magnesium ions, reporter molecules, amplification enzymes, and temperature form a coupled parameter system. Exhaustive combinatorial testing is therefore slow and costly, while conditions optimized for one target may transfer poorly to another. To address this problem, the project uses a two-way optimization loop: computational models prioritize sequences and reaction conditions, and wet-lab measurements validate and recalibrate those models.

4.1 DNABERT-6 sequence screening

A DNABERT-6 nucleic-acid language model is used to encode candidate regions as k-mers and capture PAM-adjacent context, forward/reverse primer intervals, secondary-structure effects, and longer-range nucleotide dependencies. Sequence embeddings are combined with practical thermodynamic and design features, including GC content, melting temperature, minimum free energy, conservation, and predicted off-target risk. Conserved target regions retrieved from NCBI are screened to rank compatible crRNA-primer combinations rather than optimizing each component in isolation. The resulting shortlist is then carried forward for mechanistic analysis and experimental testing.

4.2 Molecular docking and dynamics-based mechanism analysis

Candidate sequences from DNABERT-6 are evaluated against the Cas12a-crRNA-target complex using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Starting from an appropriate Cas12a structure, the analysis examines alternative PAMs, hybrid-duplex lengths, and base compositions and records conformational stability, key interactions, and binding-energy trends. These calculations do not replace functional screening; they provide mechanistic explanations for differences among candidates and help identify sequence variants that merit priority in wet-lab experiments.

4.3 Mechanistic ODE modeling of the RT-RPA-Cas12a cascade

The reaction network is represented as a set of ordinary differential equations under a well-mixed approximation. The model includes primer binding to the single-stranded DNA-binding protein Gp32, recombinase nucleation and filament assembly, reverse transcription for RNA targets, recombinase-mediated primer invasion, polymerase-driven strand extension, Cas12a-crRNA complex formation, target recognition, and reporter cleavage. Component concentrations are initialized from the experimental recipe, literature-informed rate constants are used as starting values, and the system is solved over 0-600 s with an adaptive solver such as LSODA. The simulated trajectories of amplified target, Cas12a-crRNA-target complex, and cleaved reporter link molecular events to signal rise time and endpoint fluorescence.

4.4 Wet-lab validation and DBTL iteration

Candidate crRNAs-primer pairs, and reaction formulations are tested by real-time fluorescence monitoring. Signal growth rate, endpoint intensity, analytical detection limit, and negative-control background are used to assess both sequence ranking and parameter predictions. Measurements are returned to the sequence and kinetic models to update feature weights, rate assumptions, and parameter bounds, establishing a Design-Build-Test-Learn cycle. This feedback structure is intended to reduce uninformative trial and error while retaining wet-lab evidence as the final decision criterion.

5. Portable Detection Terminal for Institutional Settings

A biological reaction can serve congregate settings only when it is incorporated into a reliable on-site workflow. The project therefore develops a lightweight POCT prototype that integrates sample pretreatment, isothermal temperature control, multi-channel fluorescence readout, result interpretation, and data export. The current engineering concept maps fluorescence acquisition and edge analysis to a custom embedded platform (Ai BOX) and uses a central rotor/sample-tube configuration with independent reaction tubes for the pathogen channels. The fluorescence module records reaction curves, while a mobile interface supports result display, record management, and authorized export. Instrument-level accuracy, channel-to-channel consistency, thermal uniformity, and field usability remain validation priorities.

6. Policy Support and Human Practices

6.1 Policy context

The intended application of this project aligns with the direction of Chinese public health policy, which emphasizes prevention, stronger primary-level capacity, and improved surveillance and early warning. The 14th Five-Year Plan for National Health calls for enhanced early monitoring, intelligent early warning, and rapid-response capacity; stronger infectious-disease prevention, control, and testing capacity in primary healthcare institutions; and improved health services for priority populations, including children and older adults [11]. The Action Plan for Improving Child Health (2021-2025) provides a policy context for earlier detection and diagnosis of childhood diseases and stronger primary-level services from the perspectives of the child health service system and the prevention and control of major diseases [12]. The Guiding Opinions on Establishing and Improving an Intelligent Multi-Point Trigger Surveillance and Early-Warning System for Infectious Diseases, issued in 2024, further propose surveillance of acute respiratory infection syndromes, multi-pathogen testing from a single sample, health monitoring in key institutions, and the development of rapid screening and testing capacity at the primary level [13]. These policies do not directly constitute approval or procurement commitments for any specific product. Collectively, however, they demonstrate a clear public health need for rapid, multi-pathogen, and traceable testing for older adults, children, and congregate settings.

6.2 Human Practices

Within the Human Practices framework, policy documents indicate only the broader direction and cannot replace the participation of actual users. At the current stage, based on the intended settings and chain of responsibility, we have identified managers of childcare and eldercare institutions, primary healthcare and disease-control professionals, laboratory and regulatory professionals, caregivers of children, and older users as core stakeholders. Their potential concerns are translated into specific indicators for subsequent interviews, usability testing, and experimental validation.

6.2.1 Institutional managers and frontline care staff

These stakeholders are particularly concerned with whether the workflow contains sufficiently few steps, whether training costs are manageable, whether results are easy to understand, and whether records can be retained rapidly when a cluster anomaly occurs. The device design therefore prioritizes reducing open-tube transfer and repeated pipetting, provides clear positive, negative, and invalid states through the interface, and maintains traceable testing records.

6.2.2 Primary healthcare and disease-control professionals

These professionals require interpretable results that can be integrated with confirmation, referral, and outbreak-reporting procedures. The system will therefore include process controls and invalid-result prompts, define a pathway for confirming positive results, and avoid presenting app-based risk notifications as clinical diagnoses.

6.2.3 Laboratory, regulatory, and biosafety professionals

These professionals are particularly concerned with analytical performance, lot-to-lot consistency, cross-contamination, sample compatibility, and quality control. Subsequent validation will therefore cover, at a minimum, the limit of detection, inclusivity and exclusivity, interference resistance, cross-reactivity, repeatability, stability, and agreement with an RT-qPCR reference method, while also evaluating closed reactions and waste-disposal procedures.

6.2.4 Caregivers, children, and older users

These stakeholders are concerned with sampling comfort, result privacy, affordability, and anxiety caused by abnormal results. The project will therefore incorporate informed communication, collection of only the minimum necessary data, permission management, and plain-language explanations of results into the product design, while avoiding diagnostic claims to non-professional users in the absence of clinical validation.

7. Project Value and Future Outlook

Using five respiratory pathogens of high public health relevance as development targets, this project integrates rapid RT-RPA amplification, sequence-specific LbCas12a recognition, DNABERT-6 screening, molecular docking and dynamics, mechanistic ODE modeling, differential-evolution parameter search, and wet-lab DBTL iteration into a continuous development pipeline. The reported RSV plasmid study provides an initial analytical proof of concept, including primer-pair selection and a 10²-copy-per-reaction detection limit for the RSV-A F2R1 and RSV-B F1R1 configurations. The core value of the project is therefore both a multi-pathogen POCT architecture and a transferable, data-driven method for developing RPA-CRISPR assays.

The portable detection platform is intended to support fluorescence-signal acquisition, embedded interpretation, and traceable reporting. Because the molecular modules and hardware are decoupled, additional targets can be incorporated after analytical verification. The next phase will prioritize specificity, repeatability, matrix-interference testing, comparison with qPCR, validation of one-step pretreatment in real respiratory samples, closed-reaction contamination control, thermal and optical calibration, multi-operator usability, and clinical agreement. Only after these studies and the relevant compliance and data-governance requirements are satisfied should integration with primary-level surveillance workflows be considered.
