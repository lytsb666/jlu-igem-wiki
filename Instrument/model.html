Computational design · structural support · kinetic search

From sequence space to a measurable signal.

RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection requires both determining the combination of RPA primers and crRNA and coordinating multiple reaction components. Therefore, our dry-lab workflow is divided into three modules: a machine learning model ranks primer-crRNA candidate combinations, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations compare structural differences among candidate combinations, and a reaction kinetics model simulates the detection process and searches for reaction conditions.

01

Why a drylab workflow

Introduction

This workflow addresses five linked design and optimization problems.

  1. 01

    Joint design

    RPA primers and crRNA are usually designed separately, and the results screened independently may not form the combination with the highest detection efficiency. We combine one pair of RPA primers and one crRNA spacer sequence into one candidate combination, and then use a machine learning model to rank the candidate combinations generated within the same target gene.

  2. 02

    Position-aware input

    Most existing guide RNA (gRNA) design models use continuous nucleic acid sequences as input. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) commonly process a single gRNA or a sequence pair composed of a gRNA and its target sequence; DNA language models such as DNABERT, Evo 2, and GENERator also use continuous sequences as their basic input. In our candidate combinations, the forward primer, reverse primer, and crRNA spacer sequence are distributed at different positions in the target gene. If the entire target gene sequence is input directly, the model cannot identify the three designed sequences in the combination; if they are concatenated before input, their original positional relationships are altered. To enable the model to identify all three designed sequences while retaining their positional relationships, we input the amplicon sequence and the three designed sequences separately into the same DNABERT-6, and then combine the numerical representations of the four sequence streams with numerical features describing sequence properties and relative positions to evaluate the complete candidate combination.

  3. 03

    Positive-only literature

    The literature generally reports only primer-crRNA combinations with good performance, which can provide positive samples, but rarely reports ineffective or poorly performing combinations, resulting in insufficient negative samples. We refer to candidate combinations used in the literature as literature combinations. To train the model under these data conditions, we first check whether each literature combination can be located at a matching position in the corresponding target gene sequence, and then generate other candidate combinations within a similar design range in the same target gene sequence. Each literature combination and its corresponding other candidate combinations form one candidate group. The machine learning model learns to rank the literature combination ahead of the other candidate combinations in the same group.

  4. 04

    Mechanistic validation

    The ranking produced by the machine learning model also requires support from molecular structures and wet-lab results. We select the top-ranked RSV candidate combinations, use molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to compare the binding conformations, intermolecular interactions, and structural stability of [the specific complex in Model 2], and measure the detection efficiency of each candidate combination in wet-lab experiments.

  5. 05

    Coupled reaction space

    In the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a reaction, the concentrations of multiple enzymes and reactants jointly affect the detection signal, and adjusting individual components one by one makes it difficult to compare a large number of concentration combinations. We establish a reaction kinetics model to describe the detection process and then use a differential evolution algorithm to search for reaction conditions within specified ranges.

To address these limitations, we established four connected computational tasks across three drylab modules.

Module 01

Rank candidates

Machine-learning screening of candidate combinations. We obtained 142 literature combinations, of which 112 completed candidate generation. The resulting 112 candidate groups contained a total of 13,552 candidate combinations. The DNABERT-6-based machine learning model combines four-stream sequence representations with 19 numerical features and scores each candidate combination. Among the 26 test candidate groups, the model ranked 24 literature combinations first in their respective candidate groups. When applied to RSV screening, the candidate combination ranked first by the model also showed the highest detection efficiency in wet-lab experiments.

Module 02

Support the mechanism

Use molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to provide mechanistic support: we performed molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations for the top 10 RSV candidate combinations and compared the binding conformations, intermolecular interactions, and structural stability of [the specific complex in Model 2].

Module 03

Simulate the cascade

Use a reaction kinetics model to search for reaction conditions: we used a set of reaction-diffusion partial differential equations to describe reverse transcription, RPA amplification, Cas12a activation, and reporter cleavage, and simulated 30 min of changes in the concentrations of the major reactants. The model uses the concentration of cleaved reporter molecules to represent the detection signal.

Module 04

Search conditions

To search for reaction conditions, we set the concentration of one pair of RPA primers and the concentrations of polymerase, recombinase, the complex formed by Cas12a and crRNA, and reverse transcriptase as the conditions to be searched. The differential evolution algorithm compares different concentration combinations within specified ranges and outputs the reaction conditions predicted by the model.

02

Two coordinated routes

Method

The three modules described above address the limitations faced by RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection through two routes. The first route consists of a machine learning model, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations and is used to screen candidate combinations and provide mechanistic support for the screening results; the second route consists of a reaction kinetics model and is used to simulate the reaction process and search for reaction conditions.

Route A

Candidate combination screening

The first route begins by using literature data to train a machine learning model. To determine the ranking that the model needs to learn when negative samples are insufficient, we generate other candidate combinations within a similar design range in the target gene sequence corresponding to each literature combination. Each literature combination for which candidate generation is completed and its corresponding other candidate combinations form one candidate group. The literature combinations have been used for actual detection in published studies, whereas the generated candidate combinations have no experimental results. We therefore make the following training assumption: within each candidate group, the target rank of the literature combination is higher than those of the other candidate combinations in the same group. All candidate groups are pooled to form the machine learning dataset. The machine learning model learns the within-group relative order described above and updates its parameters during training. After training, we use the machine learning model to screen the pathogens that the project needs to detect. Taking RSV in the first round of project testing as an example, we follow the candidate-generation process used during training-data construction to generate candidate combinations in the RSV target gene sequence, and then input these candidate combinations into the trained machine learning model to obtain their scores and rankings. We then select the top ten candidates from the ranking results, use molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to analyze the binding conformations, intermolecular interactions, and structural stability of [the specific complex in Model 2], and then conduct wet-lab validation.

03

Module 01 · machine-learning ranking

Screening primer-crRNA combinations

dataset curation

Dataset curation

To construct the dataset for the machine learning model, we searched PubMed and Google Scholar for studies on RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection published through July 30, 2026, using combinations of the search terms "RPA," "recombinase polymerase amplification," "CRISPR," "Cas12a," and "crRNA." We organized the pathogen, target gene, one pair of RPA primers, and one crRNA spacer sequence from the same detection scheme into one design record, ultimately compiling 200 data records from 103 publications for construction of the machine learning model.

We obtained the corresponding target gene sequences from NCBI GenBank according to the sequence accession numbers provided in the literature. To verify the RPA primers and crRNA spacer sequences reported in the literature, we used the locate_oligo() function in an in-house Python script to compare, one by one, target-gene subsequences of the same length as the sequence to be matched. The forward primer was matched in its original orientation, and the reverse primer was reverse-complemented before matching. Each primer was allowed a maximum of three mismatches, but the last two nucleotides at the 3′ end had to match exactly; the crRNA spacer sequence was allowed a maximum of one mismatch.

After sequence matching, we checked the orientations of the two primers, the amplicon length, the position corresponding to the crRNA spacer sequence, and its adjacent TTTV PAM, where V denotes A, C, or G. The amplicon length was restricted to 50–1500 nt, and the position corresponding to the crRNA spacer sequence had to lie between the two primers.

Among the 200 design records, all three types of sequences in 119 records exactly matched the target gene sequence, and another 23 records were matched within the specified mismatch limits, yielding 142 literature combinations. These 142 literature combinations proceeded to the subsequent candidate-generation step.

candidate generation

Candidate generation

To place the literature combinations and generated candidate combinations within similar design ranges, we used an in-house Python script to calculate the characteristics of the RPA primers, crRNA spacer sequences, and amplicons in the literature combinations, and determined the overall ranges for candidate generation accordingly. We then generated other candidate combinations in the target gene sequences corresponding to the 142 literature combinations.

To generate RPA primer pairs, we used Primer3 to design primers in the target gene sequences. For each candidate group, we set the search ranges according to the lengths, GC contents, and melting temperatures of the two primers in the corresponding literature combination. The amplicon length was uniformly restricted to 88–500 nt; other basic parameters are shown in the collapsible section below.

To generate crRNA spacer sequences, we scanned both strands of the target gene sequence for TTTV PAMs and enumerated adjacent sequences of 18–26 nt. We then used the amplification region defined by each RPA primer pair to retain sites for which the positions corresponding to both the PAM and the crRNA spacer sequence lay within that region. One pair of RPA primers and one crRNA spacer sequence satisfying the positional requirements form one candidate combination.

Among the 142 literature combinations, 28 could not produce primer pairs satisfying the specified conditions through Primer3; the primer length of one literature combination exceeded the 36-nt input limit supported by Primer3-py 0.6.1; and one target gene sequence contained non-ACGT characters and could not be processed by Primer3. The remaining 112 literature combinations formed 112 candidate groups. After removing duplicate records and organizing the generated candidate combinations, we ultimately obtained 13,552 candidate combinations, including 112 literature combinations and 13,440 generated candidate combinations.

dataset preprocessing

Dataset preprocessing

Each candidate combination contains a forward primer, a reverse primer, and a crRNA spacer sequence, but the three designed sequences are not contiguous in the target gene sequence. Inputting only the complete target gene sequence cannot explicitly identify the three sequences contained in the current candidate combination; directly concatenating the three sequences would place bases originally located at different positions together while losing the original spacing information among the three sequences. To enable the model to obtain the functional identity of each designed sequence, the nucleic acid sequence context of its region, and their relative positions simultaneously, we use the forward primer, reverse primer, and crRNA spacer sequence as separate inputs; add the amplicon sequence defined by the two primers as background input; and use numerical features to describe the positional relationships among the three designed sequences. Thus, each candidate combination forms four sequence inputs: the amplicon sequence, forward primer, reverse primer, and crRNA spacer sequence. The four sequence streams are separately converted into overlapping 6-mer sequences as inputs to DNABERT-6; detailed definitions are provided in the collapsible section below.

If the target gene sequences corresponding to candidate groups in the test set are highly similar to target gene sequences in the training set, the test results may overestimate the model's ability to generalize to new sequence contexts. To prevent similar target gene sequences from being assigned to different datasets, we clustered candidate groups according to target gene sequence similarity. Each candidate group is generated on one target gene sequence, so we use the target gene sequence corresponding to each candidate group to calculate between-group similarity. We represent each sequence as a set of 8-mers and calculate the Jaccard similarity between sets; sequences with similarity greater than or equal to 0.80 are connected, and the candidate groups corresponding to all directly or indirectly connected sequences form a homologous cluster. If a sequence does not reach this threshold of similarity with any other sequence, the candidate group corresponding to that sequence forms a homologous cluster by itself.

After clustering, we randomly selected 25% of all homologous clusters and assigned all candidate groups within them to the test set, ultimately obtaining a test set containing 26 candidate groups. The remaining 75% of homologous clusters were divided 80:20 at random into training and validation sets. Numerical features were standardized using statistics calculated from the training set, and the same transformation was applied to the validation and test sets.

model architecture

Model architecture

To integrate the four sequence inputs and 19 numerical features described above into a score for each candidate combination, we constructed a model consisting of a shared DNABERT-6 encoder, four independent sequence projection modules, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) ranking head. The model first extracts numerical representations of the four sequence inputs separately, then fuses them with the explicit numerical features, and ultimately outputs one score for each candidate combination.

The 6-mer sequences corresponding to the amplicon sequence, forward primer, reverse primer, and crRNA spacer sequence are separately input into the same DNABERT-6 encoder. For each input stream, DNABERT-6 outputs one 768-dimensional vector at each token position, forming a token-representation matrix. Based on the attention mask, we apply mean pooling to the vectors at all non-padding positions, summarizing token-representation matrices of different lengths into one 768-dimensional sequence vector for each stream.

The four 768-dimensional sequence vectors separately pass through independent linear projection layers, GELU activation functions, and layer normalization and are converted into four 64-dimensional vectors. We then concatenate the four vectors in the order of amplicon sequence, forward primer, reverse primer, and crRNA spacer sequence to obtain a 256-dimensional sequence representation. This sequence representation is further concatenated with the 19 standardized numerical features to form a 275-dimensional candidate-combination representation.

Finally, the 275-dimensional candidate-combination representation first undergoes layer normalization and then enters the MLP ranking head. The two hidden layers have dimensions of 128 and 32, respectively, and both use GELU activation functions and Dropout; the output layer generates one candidate score. Candidate combinations within the same candidate group are ranked from highest to lowest score.

model training

Model training

To enable the model to learn the relative order within each candidate group, we compare each literature combination with 120 enumerated candidates from the same group. The model calculates the candidate scores of the two candidate combinations separately and uses the difference between the two scores to calculate the pairwise ranking loss. When the candidate score of the literature combination is higher than that of the enumerated candidate, the score difference is positive and the loss decreases accordingly.

For candidate group (g), let the literature combination be (pg), the set of enumerated candidates in the same group be (Eg), and the candidate score output by the model be (s(⋅)). The loss for this candidate group is defined as:

[Lg=1|Eg|∑e∈Eglog⁡1+exp-s(pg)-s(e)]

To give each candidate group equal influence on model training, we first average all comparisons within one candidate group and then average across the candidate groups in the training set:

[L=1|Gtrain|∑g∈GtrainLg]

The training set contains 69 candidate groups, with 120 comparisons generated for each group, for a total of 8,280 comparisons. All comparisons are used in every training epoch. To control GPU memory usage, we input no more than 16 candidate combinations at a time and update the model parameters after completing all comparisons for one candidate group.

To reduce the number of trainable parameters, we freeze DNABERT-6 in the main model and train only the four sequence projection modules, layer normalization, and the MLP ranking head. The frozen DNABERT-6 remains in evaluation mode during training to prevent its Dropout from altering the sequence representations; Dropout in the MLP ranking head is enabled only during training.

We use AdamW to optimize the model parameters. The learning rate is set to 5×10⁻⁵, weight decay to 0.02, and Dropout after each of the two hidden layers of the MLP to 0.35. The gradient norm is limited to 1.0 during training. The maximum number of training epochs is set to 30, and the random seed is set to 42.

To select the training epoch, at the end of each epoch we use the 17 validation-set candidate groups to calculate Hits@1, mean reciprocal rank, and macro-averaged pairwise accuracy, and take the arithmetic mean of the three metrics as the model selection score:

MRR=1|Gtest|g∈Gtest1rank(pg)

[Sval=Hits@1+MRR+MacroPairAcc3]

Training stops when the validation-set model selection score does not improve for eight consecutive epochs. We save the parameters from the epoch with the highest validation-set model selection score and use them to evaluate the test set.

model evaluation

Model evaluation

To evaluate whether the model can rank each literature combination ahead of enumerated candidates in the same group, we input each candidate group in the test set into the trained model separately and rank candidate combinations within the group from highest to lowest candidate score. The test set contains 26 candidate groups and does not participate in model parameter updates or training-epoch selection.

Hits@1 is the proportion of candidate groups in which the literature combination ranks first. If multiple candidate combinations have the same highest score, the literature combination receives a count equal to the reciprocal of the number of candidates sharing the highest score. In addition to Hits@1, we also calculate Hits@2, Hits@3, Hits@5, and Hits@10 to determine whether the literature combination enters candidate lists of different lengths.

Hits@k=1|Gtest|g∈Gtest1[rank(pg)≤k]

Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) describes the overall position of the literature combination within the candidate group. For candidate groups without tied scores, we calculate the reciprocal of the literature combination's rank and then average across all test candidate groups. The closer the literature combination is to first place, the closer the MRR is to 1. When scores are tied, we calculate the mean reciprocal of all possible ranks of the literature combination within the tied range.

Macro-averaged pairwise accuracy evaluates whether the model can rank the literature combination ahead of enumerated candidates in the same group. We first calculate the proportion of correctly ranked comparisons within each candidate group and then average across all candidate groups. When a literature combination and an enumerated candidate have the same score, the comparison is counted as 0.5 correct. Averaging within candidate groups before averaging across groups gives each candidate group equal weight in the final metric.

ablation experiments

Ablation experiments

The machine learning model uses both four sequence inputs and 19 numerical features, with the four sequence inputs encoded by pretrained DNABERT-6. To separately examine the effects of information source, pretrained weights, and the four sequence inputs on the ranking results, we set up one complete model and eight modified models.

The complete model retains the four sequence inputs and 19 numerical features and freezes all DNABERT-6 parameters. The two information-source ablations retain only the four sequence inputs or only the 19 numerical features. The former does not input numerical features into the MLP ranking head; the latter does not use DNABERT-6 or the four sequence projection modules.

The encoder experiments included two settings. The first retained the DNABERT-6 model architecture and 6-mer vocabulary, but randomly initialized the encoder parameters and kept them frozen during training. This setting was used to compare sequence representations produced by pretrained weights with those produced by random weights. The second began training from pretrained weights, froze the encoder for the first five epochs, and then unfroze the last two Transformer layers. After unfreezing, the learning rate for these two Transformer layers was set to 5×10⁻⁶, while the learning rate for the other trainable components remained (5×10-5).

The roles of the four sequence inputs are examined by removing them one at a time. Four models respectively remove the amplicon sequence, forward primer, reverse primer, or crRNA spacer sequence and use only the remaining three sequence inputs and 19 numerical features. The removed sequence no longer enters DNABERT-6, and its corresponding sequence projection module is also omitted.

All nine models use the same data split, training comparisons, random seed, and early-stopping rule. Each model is retrained once, and the best parameters are saved according to the validation-set model selection score. After training, we calculate Hits@1, mean reciprocal rank, and macro-averaged pairwise accuracy on the same 26 test-set candidate groups.

model performance

Model performance

The validation-set model selection score reached its maximum value of 0.9060 at epoch 2. It did not exceed this value for the next eight consecutive epochs, and training ended at epoch 10. We saved the model parameters from epoch 2 and used them to evaluate the test set.

The test set contains 26 candidate groups. The model ranked the literature combination first in 24 candidate groups, while the other two literature combinations ranked 84th and 110th, respectively. Hits@1 was 0.9231, mean reciprocal rank was 0.9239, and macro-averaged pairwise accuracy was 0.9385.

ablation results

Ablation results

When only the four sequence inputs were used, Hits@1, mean reciprocal rank, and macro-averaged pairwise accuracy were 0.5000, 0.5681, and 0.8340, respectively; when only the 19 numerical features were used, the three metrics were 0.5000, 0.5703, and 0.9564, respectively. The complete model had a Hits@1 of 0.9231, higher than those of the two models using only a single information source. The model using only numerical features still had high macro-averaged pairwise accuracy, indicating that it could rank most literature combinations ahead of individual enumerated candidates but could not consistently rank the literature combination first in the candidate group.

With randomly initialized and frozen DNABERT-6, Hits@1, mean reciprocal rank, and macro-averaged pairwise accuracy were 0.9231, 0.9282, and 0.9910, respectively. This result did not show that pretrained weights produced higher ranking metrics. The model with the last two Transformer layers unfrozen obtained the same test results as the complete model, and its best parameters came from epoch 2, before the encoder had been unfrozen.

After removing the amplicon sequence, forward primer, or crRNA spacer sequence individually, Hits@1 decreased to 0.8462 in each case; after removing the reverse primer, Hits@1 decreased to 0.8077. Under the current dataset and training settings, removing any one sequence input reduced the proportion of literature combinations ranked first, with the largest decrease occurring after removal of the reverse primer.

Module 03 · reaction kinetics

RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a System Dynamics Modeling

reaction mechanism

Reaction Mechanism

We established a reaction kinetics model to describe the complete reaction pathway of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a cascade detection system from target RNA to fluorescent signal, with the aim of identifying rate-limiting steps and searching for optimal reaction conditions.

We constructed the model using RNA virus detection as an example; this reaction pathway can be extended to other nucleic acid targets.

reaction steps

Reaction Steps

The detection process is divided into four steps, each corresponding to a schematic diagram and the relevant biochemical reaction equations.

The reaction process of the RT-RPA system can be represented by the following chemical equations. In the microparticle model, the intrinsic rate constant k for every reaction below is replaced in the actual PDE solution by the apparent rate constant kapp=η⋅k，where η is determined by the local Thiele modulus.

Step 1: Recombinase-Primer Complex Synthesis

Primer F first binds reversibly to the single-stranded DNA-binding protein Gp32, forming the primer-Gp32 complex FGm. The recombinase complex R then binds to FGm, progressively displaces Gp32, and assembles on the primer to form the recombinase filament FRn, which is capable of strand invasion.

1. Binding of single-stranded DNA-binding protein to the primer

m×[Gfree]+[F]⇌[FGm]k1f,k1r

2. Nucleation and growth of the primer-recombinase complex

a.[R]+[FGm]⇌[FGmR∗]k2af,k2ar
b.[FGmR∗]→k2b[FGm−1R]+[G]
c.[FGm−1R]+(n−1)×[R]⇌[FGm−1Rn]k2cf,k2cr
d.[FGm−1Rn]→k2d[FRn]+(m−1)×[G]

Step 2: Reverse Transcription

Reverse transcriptase RT binds to the target RNA and uses the RNA as a template to catalyze synthesis of a complementary DNA (cDNA) strand, completing the transfer of information from RNA to DNA. The initial product of reverse transcription is a DNA-RNA hybrid. At the reaction temperature, the RNA and cDNA strands separate, and the released cDNA enters the subsequent amplification step.

3. RNA reverse transcription

a.[RNA]+[RT]⇌[RT−RNA]k3af,k3ar
b.[RT−RNA]+B×[dNTP]→k3b[cDNA·RNA]+[RT]+]+B×[PPi]
c.[cDNA]+[P]⇌[P−cDNA]k3cf,k3cr
d.[P−cDNA]+B×[dNTP]→k3d[P]+B×[PPi]+[DNA]

Step 3: Primer Invasion and DNA Amplification

To insert the primer into the target DNA, the recombinase filament complex FR₈ binds to double-stranded DNA and, driven by ATP hydrolysis, inserts the primer into the complementary sequence in the DNA. The other DNA strand is unwound at this point and protected by binding of the single-stranded DNA-binding protein Gp32. After insertion is completed, recombinase dissociates from the complex, forming the primer-DNA complex FD.

DNA polymerase P binds reversibly to the primer-DNA complex FD, forming the polymerase-primer-DNA complex PFD. In the presence of dNTPs, the polymerase catalyzes synthesis of the nascent DNA strand and releases pyrophosphate (PPi), ultimately yielding the amplified double-stranded DNA product.

4. Recombinase-mediated binding of DNA and primer

a.[FRn]+[DNA]+2m×[Gfree]⇌[FRnD]k4af,k4ar
b1.[FRnD]+n×ATP→k4b1cat[FD]+n×[R]+n×AMP+n×PPi

b2.[FRnD]+n×ATP→k4b2cat[FD]+n×[R]+n×ADP+H3PO4

5. Primer amplification

a.[FD]+[P]⇌[PFD]k5af,k5ar
b.[PFD]+2×B×[dNTP]→k5b[P]+2×B×[PPi]+2×[DNA]

Step 4: Cas12a Assembly and Cleavage

Cas12a assembles with pre-crRNA to form a complex, recognizes the PAM sequence of the target DNA, and activates its collateral cleavage activity, nonspecifically cleaving nearby single-stranded DNA reporter molecules to produce a detectable fluorescent signal.

6. Assembly and cleavage of the CRISPR/Cas12a system

a.[Cas12afree]+[pre−crRNA]⇌[Cas12a−pre−crRNA]k6af,k6ar
b.[Cas12a−pre−crRNA]→k6b[Cas12a−crRNA]
c.[Cas12a−crRNA]+[DNA]⇌[Cas12a−crRNA−DNA]k6cf,k6cr
d.[Cas12a−crRNA−DNA]+[Repoter]→k6d[Cas12a−crRNA−DNA]+X

model assumptions

Model Assumptions

Reaction Pathway

To simplify the model, we assume that reactions proceed in a defined order during the initial stage: Gp32 first binds to the primer, after which recombinase nucleates stepwise and forms a complete filament; in RT-RPA, RNA is first reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by RPA amplification; Cas12a cleavage occurs after DNA amplification has produced sufficient substrate.

Given the asymmetry in the reaction kinetics of the forward and reverse primers, we introduce a primer efficiency coefficient ξ and multiply by this coefficient in the differential equations to simplify the description.

Parameter Values

Stoichiometric parameters are determined according to molecular binding characteristics: each primer binds four Gp32 molecules (32-nt primer, with each Gp32 molecule covering 8 nt) and eight recombinase monomers (each UvsX monomer binds 4 bp).

Kinetic Conditions

The Michaelis-Menten steady-state assumption is not applicable to recombinase-mediated primer insertion; we therefore describe this step using a basic two-step reaction.

The initial amount of substrate in the reaction system is limited, so the model explicitly accounts for the effect of substrate depletion on reaction rates.

System State and Mass Transfer

We use a microparticle model to describe mass-transfer effects in the heterogeneous reaction system, introducing the effects of spatial concentration gradients and molecular diffusion.

In this model, the concentration of each species is a function of time and spatial position, Cᵢ(r,t), rather than only the overall mean concentration, Cᵢ(t). The behavior of the system is described by reaction-diffusion partial differential equations (PDEs):

∂Ci∂t=Di∇2Ci+Ri(C1,C2,…,Cn)

where:

Cᵢ(r,t) is the concentration of species i at position r and time t;

Dᵢ is the effective diffusion coefficient of species i in the reaction medium (unit: μm²/s);

Rᵢ is the chemical reaction source term determined by the law of mass action (unit: nM/s).

For spherical microparticles (radius Rp), the diffusion equation in spherical coordinates is used, and the radial concentration gradient ∂Ci∂r becomes a key variable. In the spherical coordinate system, the Laplace operator is:

∇2Ci=1r2∂∂rr2∂Ci∂r

Mass Transfer Processes

The model explicitly considers three types of mass-transfer processes:

External mass transfer (film diffusion): substrate passes from the bulk solution through the diffusion boundary layer at the particle surface to reach the microsphere surface;

Internal mass transfer (pore diffusion): substrate diffuses through pores inside the microsphere to reach immobilized enzymes or binding sites;

Convection-diffusion coupling: under stirring conditions, the bulk solution remains uniform, but a concentration gradient still exists at the microsphere surface.

The boundary conditions are set as follows:

Microsphere center (r = 0): symmetry boundary condition, ∂Ci∂r=0.

Microsphere surface (r = R_p): accounting for external mass-transfer resistance, −Di∂Ci∂r=km,i(Ci,b−Ci,s), where km,i is the external mass-transfer coefficient, Ci,b is the bulk concentration, and Ci,s is the surface concentration.

effectiveness factor

Effectiveness Factor and Thiele Modulus

The effectiveness factor (Effectiveness Factor) η is introduced to quantify the effect of mass-transfer limitations on the reaction rate:

η=actual apparent reaction rateintrinsic reaction rate without diffusion limitation

For the key reaction steps in this system, the relationship between the apparent rate constant kapp and the intrinsic rate constant kint is:

kapp=η⋅kint

Here, η is determined by the Thiele modulus ϕ. For spherical particles and Michaelis-Menten kinetics, the general form of ϕ is:

ϕ=RpVmax(Km⋅Deff)

For a first-order reaction, the specific expression for the Thiele modulus is:

ϕ=RpkintDeff

For spherical particles, the relationship between the effectiveness factor and the Thiele modulus is approximated by:

η=1ϕ1tanh⁡(3ϕ)−13ϕ

When ϕ≪1, η≈1 (reaction-controlled, without diffusion limitations); when ϕ≫1, η≪1 (diffusion-controlled, with severe mass-transfer limitations).

Enzyme Activity

RT: RT is primarily involved in cDNA generation during the early stage of the reaction. Although RT may undergo time-dependent thermal inactivation at temperatures near 42°C, its main contribution to system initiation is concentrated in the early stage of the reaction. To reduce the number of parameters in the base model, and because RT thermal-inactivation parameters are strongly affected by the enzyme type, buffer system, and template-binding state, the base model temporarily assumes that the total effective activity of RT remains constant over 30 min and does not explicitly include an RT inactivation term. However, RT activity is affected by diffusion limitations, and its apparent rate constant is k3afapp=ηRT⋅k3af

R: UvsX has extremely high intrinsic ATP hydrolysis activity. Without perfect buffering by an energy-regeneration system, ADP accumulated over 30 min strongly competitively inhibits UvsX, causing functional inactivation after prolonged incubation; we introduce a damping coefficient γ to represent the inhibitory effect of ADP on R. At the same time, mass-transfer limitations on recombinase at the microsphere surface are corrected by the effectiveness factor ηR.

P: The commercial DNA polymerase used in the experiment is suitable for the RT-RPA/Cas12a reaction system and can maintain good stability over the short 30-min reaction course. The base model temporarily assumes that the total effective activity of P remains constant over 30 min and does not explicitly include a P inactivation term. Mass-transfer limitations on the polymerase reaction are corrected by the effectiveness factor ηP.

Cas12a: Once Cas12a assembles with crRNA, its three-dimensional conformation becomes tightly locked. It is highly stable at 37°C, with a half-life far exceeding 120 min; therefore, its thermal inactivation can be completely neglected during the short 30-min detection cycle. However, diffusion of the Cas12a-crRNA complex in the pores of the microsphere and its recognition of the DNA substrate are subject to diffusion limitations; the effectiveness factor ηCas is introduced to correct the apparent cleavage rate.

simulation results

Simulation Results

We set the initial conditions and performed numerical simulations. The initial concentrations of all species other than the specified components were set to 0. Spatial discretization was performed using the method of lines, together with the LSODA solver, to simulate system dynamics over 30 min.

Initiation Phase (0–5 min)

The DNA substrate (blue line) rises rapidly and reaches an early peak, indicating rapid generation of target DNA. The Cas12a-crRNA-DNA complex (purple dotted line) and Cas12a-crRNA (green dashed line) rise simultaneously, indicating that Cas12a assembly and activation occur almost immediately after substrate generation.

Amplification Phase (5–25 min)

After activation, Cas12a develops nonspecific cleavage activity, cleaves the single-stranded DNA reporter molecules, and accumulates product X; signal X (red line) enters a rapid-growth interval.

Quasi-steady Phase (25–30 min)

The DNA substrate (blue line) gradually decreases and levels off, while the Cas12a-crRNA-DNA complex (purple dotted line) and Cas12a-crRNA (green dashed line) remain at relatively high levels. The system enters a quasi-steady output state dominated by sustained cleavage; the final signal X (red line) continues to accumulate monotonically and gradually approaches a plateau.

The RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a cascade system completes the entire process from substrate generation to signal accumulation on a minute timescale.

condition optimization

Condition Optimization

Optimization Approach

To search for optimal reaction conditions, we use a differential evolution algorithm to perform global optimization within the specified parameter space. The concentration of signal product X at 10 min is used as the objective function, while the concentrations of six key components are adjusted simultaneously: primer F, recombinase R, polymerase P, Gp32, the Cas12a-crRNA complex, and reverse transcriptase RT.

Key Components Analysis

The roles of the key components are analyzed as follows:

Reverse transcriptase RT determines the delay before reaction initiation. Increasing the RT concentration can accelerate cDNA synthesis and shorten the reaction initiation time.

Recombinase R directly affects RPA amplification efficiency. Optimizing its concentration can increase the amplification rate and thereby accelerate signal amplification.

The Cas12a concentration directly determines the generation efficiency of signal X. Increasing the concentration can accelerate cleavage and improve sensitivity.

DNA polymerase P determines strand-extension efficiency. Optimizing its concentration can ensure the extension rate during amplification.

Optimization Results

We use a differential evolution algorithm for two-stage optimization: in the first stage, a low-precision PDE configuration is used for a global coarse search to identify high-potential regions; in the second stage, the configuration is switched to high precision for a fine search within the candidate regions.
