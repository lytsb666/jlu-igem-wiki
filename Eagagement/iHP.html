Human Practices

From voices in the community to decisions at the bench—and back again.

HP

Science shaped by the people it serves

Human Practices was not a final checkpoint. It was our design engine.

Respiratory infections are strongly seasonal, spread rapidly, and can cause severe complications. Older adults and young children are especially vulnerable. For families, illness brings physical discomfort, anxiety, disrupted routines, and the risk of cross-infection in crowded clinical settings.

We set out to develop a portable, user-friendly platform for simultaneous, accurate detection of five respiratory pathogens. Its purpose is larger than a single test: it is designed to support rapid community response and contribute to a grassroots early-warning network.

Laboratory performance alone cannot make a technology useful. Across the year, public-health experts, clinicians, caregivers, teachers, parents, families, manufacturers, and potential users influenced our target profile, hardware, sampling, interpretation, biosafety, accessibility, and deployment model.

Our working principle

Listen before designing. Test assumptions in context. Return feedback to the project.

Who shapes the project

Stakeholders

Listen to stakeholders’ voices, and root science in real life

Since project initiation, we have worked beyond an isolated laboratory perspective, grounding our decisions in real application settings and in the people who may shape or be shaped by the project. Field visits, interviews, public-health expertise, clinical practice, social value, and ethical safeguards became one connected decision system.

Our Integrated Human Practices framework moves through four linked stages. Each stage below opens the corresponding evidence trail, so the stakeholder analysis remains a visible thread through the whole project rather than a one-off consultation.

Core stakeholders6 profiles

Patients

Patients need timely, understandable results and a clear boundary between screening and diagnosis. Their experience informs sampling comfort, interface language, privacy, affordability, and referral guidance.

Susceptible populations

Older adults, young children, and people with underlying conditions face higher risk of severe outcomes. Their needs make accessibility, gentle sampling, speed, and supervision central design requirements.

Close contacts

Families, classmates, co-residents, and caregivers are affected by uncertainty and isolation decisions. They benefit from rapid screening, transparent interpretation, and proportionate follow-up.

Nursing homes and kindergartens

These high-density care settings can function as sentinel nodes. They require simple operation, training, stock management, cluster escalation rules, and workflows that do not overload staff.

Public-health agencies

Agencies shape surveillance standards, staff training, data quality, and escalation. Their feedback introduced the 30-minute target, multiplexing, and secure interoperability as system-level requirements.

Medical institutions

Clinical laboratories and frontline departments help validate analytical performance, identify low-copy challenges, define referral boundaries, and keep the platform aligned with clinical reality.

Direct stakeholders3 profiles

Co-manufacturers

Manufacturing partners translate prototypes into reproducible hardware and consumables. They bring requirements for quality control, cost, traceability, delivery, and maintainability.

Dealers

Dealers connect packaging, logistics, after-sales service, and regional market feedback. Their data can complete a production–sales–service improvement loop.

Health-insurance payers

Payers influence access and affordability. Engagement should focus on cost accounting, health-economic evidence, and a realistic position for screening within existing care pathways.

Indirect stakeholders4 profiles

The public

Public trust determines whether a technology is understood and used responsibly. Communication must explain capabilities, limits, privacy, and appropriate follow-up without amplifying fear.

Educational institutions

Schools and universities support age-adapted health education, science communication, and long-term public literacy about respiratory disease and synthetic biology.

Supervision departments

Regulators and supervisors define safety, evidence, quality, and market-access expectations. Early consultation reduces the risk of building toward an unsuitable pathway.

Policy makers

Policy makers determine whether community surveillance, data exchange, and local preparedness models can fit public-health priorities and legal safeguards.

Trace the connection context

Stakeholders are valuable because of their relationships—not only their labels.

01Core stakeholders: an integrated multi-actor network

Patients, vulnerable groups, close contacts, care settings, public-health agencies, and medical institutions form the central information and action network. A result begins with the person being tested, but its meaning depends on staff operation, clinical interpretation, referral pathways, and public-health context.

The design therefore links user experience with institutional response: gentle sampling and clear results at the front end, clinical boundaries and escalation at the back end.

02Direct stakeholders: a commercial closed loop

Co-manufacturers convert design files into controlled production; dealers transmit packaging, service, and regional demand information; payers influence cost and access. Regular communication can connect research, manufacturing, sales, quality traceability, and user feedback.

03Organic linkage between direct and core stakeholders

Manufacturing choices affect whether caregivers can operate the device, whether cartridges remain affordable, and whether replacement and service are practical. Conversely, feedback from patients, institutions, and public-health agencies becomes the basis for production specifications and service priorities.

04Interrelations among indirect stakeholders

Policy, supervision, education, and public communication jointly shape the conditions for adoption. Oversight supports trust; education supports comprehension; public feedback exposes uncertainty; policy aligns resources and responsibilities.

05Direct and indirect stakeholders working hand in hand

Manufacturers and dealers need regulatory guidance and public-health context. Educational partners can translate technical features into accessible language, while policy and supervision departments help clarify evidence, data, and quality expectations.

06Synergy between indirect and core stakeholders

Public education supports responsible use, while frontline needs reveal where policy or guidance is incomplete. Community evidence can inform better preparedness, and oversight can protect the people whose data and care are involved.

A transparent scoring basis helped us decide who needs close collaboration, consultation, information, or monitoring.

Method

Two Delphi rounds, six scorers, ten indicators

Our panel included two team members, one public-health expert, one medical expert, and two target users. Power was assessed through decision participation, resource control, information advantage, organisational hierarchy, and social influence. Interest was assessed through potential gains or losses, strategic coupling, risk exposure, issue attention, and sunk cost.

  • Round 2: rescoring after structured feedback.

Nursing homes and kindergartens inspired a dual-sentinel model. Their environments bring dense contact networks, vulnerable users, and operational constraints. Empathy Engineering converted those realities into a locking slot, a large high-contrast interface, voice assistance, and a soft lollipop-style sampler concept.

The source narrative records a survey and interviews involving 755 eldercare workers. Their concerns centred on speed, simplicity, non-invasive sampling, multiplexing, workflow fit, cost, training, and equipment. The instrument itself contains questions rather than response data, so we do not publish unsupported percentages.

Medical and public-health experts then connected the front-end experience to low-copy amplification, target selection, validation, and potential community reporting. The device remains an auxiliary screening tool; clinical diagnosis and treatment decisions belong to qualified professionals.

Part 4

Reflection and summary

Reviewing and Adapting

The stakeholder map is a living model. New evidence should be able to correct our assumptions.

We used stakeholder feedback to expose gaps, challenge bias, and return divergent viewpoints to the project’s top-level design. Periodic review keeps engagement aligned with the people and settings the platform is meant to serve.

Our aim is not a single round of consultation. It is a durable two-way mechanism in which design changes are documented, tested, and reopened when evidence changes.

Where the project began

Project Topic Origin

Responding to the challenge of rapid, on-site detection for emerging and outbreak respiratory disease.

Listening in Changchun

A need defined outside the laboratory

We began with community interviews and questionnaires, then visited settings where delayed answers are especially disruptive: nursing homes and kindergartens. These conversations reframed a technical question—how to detect several pathogens at once—as a public-health question: how can a result be fast, affordable, understandable, and useful at the point where decisions must be made?

01
Public dialogueWhat people asked us to prioritise

The public did not ask for complexity. They asked for a result they could trust and understand. Four recurring requirements guided the project:

  • Start from real health needs rather than technical novelty.
  • Pair innovation with clear public-health education.
  • Balance simplicity, accuracy, speed, and affordability.
  • Design for communities, nursing homes, kindergartens, and families—not only laboratories.
02
Nursing homeWhen the cause is unclear, the whole floor may have to close

Care staff described how a respiratory outbreak can move through an entire floor within a week. Without rapid identification, institutions may resort to broad closed management, increasing anxiety and workload. Their practical brief was direct: the device should be affordable, fast, accurate, easy for caregivers to operate, and compatible with routine monitoring.

We responded by focusing on age-friendly sampling, clear visual guidance, voice assistance, and a sentinel workflow that could escalate clusters without turning the device into a stand-alone diagnosis.

03
KindergartenMake early decisions without creating panic

Teachers explained that young children often cannot describe discomfort clearly. A fever can trigger class-wide pickup and uncertainty for families. A simple, rapid differentiator could support earlier referrals and reduce unnecessary disruption.

This reinforced the need for non-intimidating sampling, minimal steps, accessible instructions, and strong adult supervision.

04
Expert validationDialogue with Dean Li Juan

Dean Li Juan confirmed that delayed respiratory-pathogen identification is not only a clinical inconvenience but also a public-health challenge. Her feedback sharpened the project objective: move from an interesting platform to an effective, portable, and carefully bounded testing aid with a target sample-to-result time of approximately 30 minutes.

05
Science communicationBreathe with Peace of Mind

We translated the project concept into a song and concept video shared through Bilibili and YouTube. Music allowed the team to communicate the emotional reason behind the technology: caregivers and families do not simply need more data; they need reassurance, clarity, and a path to action. The work became an emotional manifesto for a community-centred diagnostic system.

What changed

The topic evolved from “build a multiplex detector” to “build a trustworthy community tool”: user-first operation, a balance of speed and accuracy, privacy-aware interpretation, and a possible link between local sentinel nodes and wider public-health surveillance.

03

Expert-guided iteration

Project Content

Two dialogue tracks—system-level public health and frontline clinical practice—converged on one product brief.

Four CDC experts across China

Three non-negotiable system requirements

  1. Time: target a result within 30 minutes.
  2. Coverage: detect multiple pathogens in a single workflow.
  3. Connection: make data secure and potentially interoperable with public-health systems.

The feedback led us to define not only a device but also a proposed Community Collaborative Emergency Response Guide. The guide remains a project proposal, not an approved clinical or regulatory standard.

Epidemiology and statistics

Vice Dean Changgui Kou

His dialogue connected pathogen prioritisation with epidemiological burden, bacterial/viral differentiation, and antibiotic stewardship. It pushed the team to examine conserved sequences and the practical challenge of handling both RNA and DNA targets.

Clinical laboratory

Director Wang

Low pathogen loads can produce signals below the threshold of a direct assay. This feedback moved the platform toward nucleic-acid amplification combined with CRISPR-Cas12a rather than recognition alone.

Infectious disease and respiratory care

Physicians and nurses

Frontline staff asked for foolproof operation, traffic-light interpretation, multiplexing, portability, battery life, and tolerance of variable working conditions. Their feedback influenced microfluidic integration, minimal app steps, and a rugged enclosure.

Geriatric care

Age-friendly redesign

The geriatric consultation highlighted fragile mucosa, the risk of rigid swabs, small text, delicate controls, impaired vision, and hand tremors. We responded with a gentler throat-sampling concept, larger interface elements, stronger contrast, and voice guidance.

Editorial transparency: the supplied drafts record different target panels at different project stages. The Project Design draft specifies hMPV, RSV, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Bordetella pertussis, and Haemophilus influenzae; another stakeholder draft lists influenza, COVID-19, RSV, hMPV, and S. pneumoniae. We therefore present the panel as an evolving design decision and do not imply that a final locked panel has been clinically validated.

Validating the pathway

From sequence semantics to complete reaction kinetics

Four molecular-diagnostics dialogues converted broad expert advice into a closed-loop computational and experimental pathway.

  1. 01 · Assay architecture

    Professor X. Chris Le

    Recommended a one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a route to reduce equipment dependence and better fit portable use than conventional PCR or RT-qPCR.

  2. 02 · Sequence screening

    Professor Quanshun Li

    Introduced DNABERT-6 sequence-language modelling, diverse positive and negative training data, PAM scanning, and similarity-controlled data splitting.

  3. 03 · Structural evaluation

    Professor Xing-Fang Li

    Added molecular docking and dynamics to evaluate Cas12a/crRNA compatibility, binding energy, thermodynamics, and RNP stability before wet-lab prioritisation.

  4. 04 · System kinetics

    Professor Weiwei Cui

    Extended evaluation to full RPA–CRISPR kinetics and ODE-based optimisation, connecting sequence selection, structural performance, and reaction conditions.

Design loop

Biological semantic screening → molecular-dynamics evaluation → full-reaction kinetic simulation → wet-lab evidence → updated screening criteria.

05

Responsible by design

The CARES Shield

Ethics is not a decorative statement. Each principle is connected to a design or research safeguard.

CACARESRES
C

Compassion & Humanistic Care

Reduce fear with plain-language and comic-style guidance; use high-contrast text, voice announcements, and community education that treats anxiety as a real design input.

A

Autonomy & Informed Consent

Use age-appropriate assent, guardian or proxy consent where required, voluntary recording, withdrawal rights, data minimisation, anonymisation, encrypted storage, and planned deletion.

R

Reliability & Transparency

State intended use and limitations, separate screening from diagnosis, plan independent validation, and communicate uncertainty. We do not repeat unverified approval or patent claims from the draft as established facts.

E

Equity & Accessibility

Prioritise low-power operation, large visual elements, audible feedback, special-population outreach, and formats such as tactile models and Braille resources.

S

Safety

Avoid live-organism participation in public activities, select safer materials, prevent open-cartridge contamination, and treat AI interpretation as support that must be checked against validated performance.

Kindergarten activity

Assent, supervision, and media choice

The draft framework requires child verbal assent, guardian authorisation for identifiable media, teacher supervision, safe materials, the right to withdraw, anonymisation, encrypted storage, and a defined destruction date.

Nursing-home usability study

Voluntary participation and proxy protection

The draft protocol defines optional audio/video, a 20–40-minute interaction, anonymisation, encrypted storage, withdrawal without penalty, a non-diagnostic disclaimer, and a proxy route when appropriate.

The supplied consent forms are unsigned drafts and contain personal contact fields. To protect participants and avoid presenting incomplete records as approvals, they are summarised here but are not offered for public download.

Proposed workflowCommunity Collaborative Emergency Response Guide

The supplied guide is a project proposal rather than an approved standard. It outlines how community health centres, elderly-care institutions, childcare institutions, and local disease-control agencies could coordinate:

  1. Establish trained sentinel nodes in high-density care settings.
  2. Initiate on-site sampling when acute respiratory-infection indicators appear, targeting a result within 30 minutes.
  3. Apply pathogen-specific follow-up: isolation and disinfection language for selected viral positives; vital-sign monitoring for high-risk RSV or hMPV cases; clinical referral for bacterial positives.
  4. Use a proposed cluster trigger of three epidemiologically linked positives for the same pathogen at one node within 24 hours.
  5. Transmit encrypted, de-identified information through authorised systems with a designated liaison.
  6. Maintain proposed reserves for 14 days of full-load routine operation and a 30-day winter/spring baseline.

These thresholds, data flows, and clinical actions require professional, privacy, regulatory, and local public-health review before implementation.
