01

The starting point

Why HEART-Edu Loop?

Explore 24 entriesSee the learning loop

Respiratory-health information is often fragmented precisely where it matters most. The gap is not simply whether people have encountered scientific knowledge. It is whether they can access information that is credible, understandable, and actionable within their own lives. New parents may worry about infant infections while navigating the fragmented routines of postpartum care. Staff in care settings need to translate abstract guidance into everyday practice. Children often need to touch, play with, and create representations of an unseen microbial world. People who encounter sensory, communication, or participation barriers need scientific communication that can genuinely fit their pace of life.

For this reason, we did not approach education as a series of isolated talks. We developed HEART-Edu Loop as an ongoing, audience-responsive cycle. Its three communities provide a practical starting point. Health Guardians are people who support health-vulnerable groups, provide care, or make key health decisions. Biotech Explorers are learners who can engage more deeply with synthetic biology through exploration, creativity, discussion, and communication. Diversity Partners remind us that science communication must account for differences in accessibility, communication, and participation.

Rather than applying one set of materials to every audience, we began with context: what setting people were in, what questions they could reasonably ask there, and what forms of interaction would support participation. Questions, participant-created work, questionnaires, interviews, and field observations became inputs for subsequent educational design. In this way, knowledge was not only delivered; it was reorganized through dialogue. Education did not only reach communities—it also changed how we designed materials and how we understood public hopes and concerns around synthetic biology.

Scientific foundation

From respiratory pathogens to educational priorities

Our project targets five respiratory pathogens: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus subtype B (hMPV-B), Streptococcus pneumoniae (SPN), Bordetella pertussis (BP), and Haemophilus influenzae (HI). The panel comprises two viral targets and three bacterial targets. Their transmission patterns and burdens differ, but public-health evidence consistently identifies infants and young children, older adults, pregnant people, and some people with underlying conditions or weakened immune systems as groups for whom respiratory infections can involve elevated risks, care burdens, or complex health decisions.[3] [4] [6] [7] [8]

This scientific background explains why education about respiratory health should enter infant-care, pregnancy, early-childhood, eldercare, and caregiving settings. It does not, however, determine the format of an educational activity on its own. Epidemiology can explain why a community deserves particular attention; it cannot fully explain how a leaflet, a waiting-room interaction, a story, a movement-based activity, or a supported craft session should be designed. We therefore combined this background with conversations with partner institutions, observations in activity settings, and participant feedback. Relevant community needs, use settings, and project-design inputs are also connected to our Human Practices work.

Our use of the term Health Guardians is not a medical classification of an individual’s risk. It describes a person’s position in information sharing, risk communication, and everyday care. Likewise, Biotech Explorers and Diversity Partners are not organized by disease severity. They are organized around opportunities to participate in synthetic biology, and around the conditions that make such participation more accessible.

Scope statement. The epidemiological information on this page explains educational priorities and public-health context. It is not intended to assess any individual’s infection or severe-disease risk and does not constitute medical advice. Our educational materials introduce scientific concepts and our project’s research direction; they do not present an investigational prototype as a clinically validated diagnostic or therapeutic service.

RSV is a major cause of lower-respiratory infection in children under five; infants under six months and older adults can face elevated severe-disease risk.

This bacterium can colonize the nasopharynx and cause pneumonia, bloodstream infection, and meningitis, with particular concern for children, older adults, and immunocompromised people.

Priority: vaccination and protective-health communication

This bacterium can cause respiratory and other infections. Despite its name, it does not cause influenza; the current panel targets HI rather than influenza viruses.[8]

The same logic operates within individual activities. Each activity begins with a specific community and setting, uses an interaction method appropriate to that setting, and creates space for participants to express questions, interpretations, or preferences. We do not treat the number of activities as educational impact in itself. What matters is whether an activity enables people to ask, create, question, and take part in public conversations around synthetic biology.

04

Part I · Health Guardians

Turning information into care that can be acted upon

Health Guardians includes people who support health-vulnerable groups, undertake caregiving responsibilities, or make important health decisions. We did not assume that these communities only needed more information. They needed materials, pacing, and interactions that could fit everyday care. Our activities therefore began in concrete settings—maternity wards, waiting rooms, postpartum-care centers, kindergartens, and eldercare facilities—where scientific information could become understandable, discussable, and portable.

H01Care in action

Infant parents: pneumococcal vaccine-protection outreach

March 28, Changchun Maternity Hospital

Design

To enhance parents' understanding of respiratory health protection for infants and knowledge related to vaccines, and to provide a starting point for subsequent consultations.

Build & Test

We used concise leaflets, small posters, and one-to-one conversations to support new parents in understanding basic links between infant respiratory health and pneumococcal disease. Materials were organized around three practical questions: why the issue deserves attention, what everyday protective measures look like, and when professional advice should be sought. We intentionally avoided overly technical language.

Feedback & Learn

We asked parents to read the leaflet independently, find information on where to seek advice, circle words or phrases they found difficult, and explain one prevention recommendation in their own words. Volunteers noted where parents needed help finding information and collected their suggestions. We chose this reading exercise to see whether the material was still easy to use without someone explaining it in person. It showed us that helping parents find the information they needed was as important as making the leaflet comprehensive. Technical terms and small print were recurring difficulties when parents looked for consultation guidance and identified unclear passages. Some understood the basic message about prevention but still needed a volunteer's help to find out where to seek further advice. This reminded us that materials for families with newborns need to accommodate brief, interrupted reading and make common questions easy to find. We plan to reorder the leaflet, explain essential terms in everyday language, and make consultation information more prominent. After revising it, we will invite parents who have not seen the original to read it and observe whether they can find answers independently, so that the leaflet remains useful after the explanation at the event has ended.

H08Care in action

Expectant mothers: “A First Safety Radar for Your Baby” waiting-room micro-engagement

March 28, Changchun Maternity Hospital

Design

To introduce the project's research focus and baseline respiratory health data in a low-intrusive, contactless manner, while listening to the real-life concerns of pregnant families.

Build & Test

Pregnancy waiting rooms are information-dense spaces with limited dwell time and substantial privacy needs. We used audio, leaflets, QR codes, and an anonymous message channel to open a low-disruption conversation about family respiratory health, testing concepts, and newborn-care concerns.

Feedback & Learn

We provided anonymous feedback cards in the waiting area, asking participants to select their preferred information format, write down questions about protecting their families, and describe their understanding of the project's research stage. Volunteers collected the cards without interfering with appointment calls. We chose brief feedback that participants could stop at any time to fit around their wait and give those who preferred not to speak directly a way to respond. Comments and feedback cards showed that personal concerns, such as family protection and newborn care, offered an accessible starting point for discussion. Some participants welcomed a short introduction; others preferred to read quietly or learn more after their appointment. Responses about the project's stage also reminded us to explain how far the work had progressed when presenting the testing concept, so that the model did not raise expectations beyond its scope. We therefore plan to condense the core information into a short text, state the research stage at the beginning, and offer audio and further discussion as options for those who want to learn more. Ending conversations when participants are called and allowing them to leave at any time will also be included in the activity arrangements. Feedback from the waiting area made it clearer that information's usefulness depends closely on whether people can engage with it at their own pace.

H02Care in action

Postpartum-care staff: pneumococcal vaccine mini-class

March 20, Binaien Postpartum Care Center

Design

To help staff provide more accurate and human-centered communication when addressing parents' questions.

Build & Test

Postpartum-care staff often occupy an important communication position between newborn families and health information. We held a mini-class using illustrated materials, knowledge questions, and scenario-based discussion to translate pneumococcal-health content into language that could be used in care conversations.

Feedback & Learn

We paired staff members and asked them to take turns playing a parent with a vaccination question and the person responding. Observers recorded whether they explored the parent's concerns, distinguished general information from judgments about an individual case, and clearly explained where to seek advice. After discussing the exchange, each pair practiced a different scenario. We chose role-play to see whether participants could put their knowledge into words when speaking with parents. The exercise made the difficulties staff encountered in everyday conversations more specific. When faced with a parent's vaccination question, participants generally knew that further professional advice was needed, but sometimes offered only a broad reminder and struggled to explain what the parent could do next. During practice after the group discussion, the distinction between general information and individual judgment became clearer, and guidance on seeking advice became more specific. This showed us that classroom knowledge needs to be practiced in conversation before it can be used in care communication. We plan to retain common parental questions in future materials, alongside prompts for exploring concerns, explaining general information, and setting out next steps, so that staff can use them for practice and reference. Points left unclear during the scenarios will also become priorities for future classes.

H03Care in action

Children aged 3–5: seven-step handwashing education

April 27, Jilin Provincial No. 2 Kindergarten

Design

To encourage children to wash their hands willingly, to teach them the correct way to wash their hands, and to help them remember the key times to wash their hands.

Build & Test

At Jilin Provincial No. 2 Kindergarten, we used a cartoon “germ” story, child-friendly slides, a movement mnemonic, and step-by-step imitation to introduce the seven-step handwashing method. The activity translated invisible microbes into characters and movements that young children could recognize and practice.

Feedback & Learn

During classroom practice, teachers asked children to demonstrate the handwashing movements independently. They observed first, then gave brief prompts about any areas the children had missed, recording which movements were completed independently and which required prompting. We chose to observe actions because practical tasks are well suited to showing young children's understanding and reduce the influence of reading and speaking skills on feedback. Classroom records showed that stories and demonstrations helped children engage in handwashing practice, while individual guidance gradually helped them perform the movements independently. This left us with a question worth following up: would children remember the steps they could perform in class when washing their hands in daily life? We plan to work with teachers to collect further feedback during children's actual handwashing, noting commonly missed areas and movements that still need verbal reminders. Illustrations beside the sinks and the movement cues used in class will also be adjusted based on these observations. What children do independently and where they need help can give teachers specific clues for teaching. This approach can connect a group lesson with daily kindergarten practice and help us decide which explanations and movement cues are worth keeping.

H04Care in action

Young children: modular clay workshop

April 27, Jilin Provincial No. 2 Kindergarten

Design

To help children gain an initial understanding of bacteriophages, antibodies, and functional enzymes through modular creative activities.

Build & Test

Phages, antibodies, and functional enzymes are difficult for young children to understand through language alone. In this workshop, different biological components became clay modules that children could make, display, and discuss. Color, shape, and combination provided an initial bridge between structure and function.

Feedback & Learn

We asked children to explain how the modules connected as they built their models, then point out what the colors and bases represented. Volunteers recorded which choices children made independently and where adults helped, and listened as children introduced their work to peers. We combined observation of the making process with children's explanations to understand both what they did and how they understood their designs. Children showed interest in choosing and combining clay modules and could use their models to describe how different parts connected. Their explanations also revealed an interpretation introduced by the models: some children assumed that the colors represented the actual colors of biological structures. Observing the process showed that a finished-looking model could involve considerable adult help, making it difficult to understand a child's own ideas from the final piece alone. This feedback led us to pay closer attention to children's explanations and choices while creating. In the next workshop, we plan to demonstrate that the same structure can be made in different colors and ask children to explain their choices as they build, making the purposes of the colors and bases clearer. We also hope to retain records of independent work and prompted steps so that presentations show children's thinking as well as the models they have made.

H05Care in action

Young children: “Magic Bio-Bricks”

April 27, Jilin Provincial No. 2 Kindergarten

Design

Use the "building with Lego" analogy to help children gain an initial understanding of DNA, the assembly of life components, and synthetic biology.

Build & Test

“Magic Bio-Bricks” used children’s familiarity with building toys to introduce basic ideas about DNA, genetic components, and synthetic biology. We used concrete expressions such as “the instruction book of life” and “bio-chefs” to explain that scientists can combine components to explore new functions.

Feedback & Learn

We asked children to identify the analogy used for DNA in class from a set of pictures, choose a diagram showing components working together, and name or point to a possible use. We then used illustrated scenarios to ask whether a new design could be used immediately. Children could answer aloud or point to pictures, while volunteers recorded their responses and any prompts. We chose picture tasks so that children with limited reading skills or those reluctant to speak could also express their understanding. Picture choices and responses after the class showed that familiar comparisons with instruction manuals and building blocks helped children approach the concepts of DNA and combining components. They could suggest things they wanted to achieve with biological building blocks, but the distinction between imagining a design and validating it remained unclear when they discussed whether it could be used immediately. This made us realize that science outreach for young children needs to explain the process of scientific exploration, with testing and checking included in the story. We plan to use a sequence of illustrated scenarios, asking children to describe how an idea goes through trials and checks before discussing whether it is suitable for use. Pointing to and arranging pictures will remain response options, giving children who prefer not to speak room to express themselves. The feedback helped us adjust both the content of our explanations and the ways children could join the discussion.

H06Care in action

Older adults: visible protection

April 10, Zhiai Eldercare Medical Facility

Design

To convey basic safety precautions through large-print materials and straightforward explanations, while fostering emotional connection and health awareness through postcards.

Build & Test

At Zhiai Eldercare Medical Facility, we used large-font, high-contrast slides, paced explanations, and one-to-one conversation. The activity combined group communication in a common room with supported conversations for people with reduced mobility, making information easier to read, hear, and revisit.

Feedback & Learn

We asked older adults to explain an everyday prevention practice in their own words, noting whether they needed to consult the material or have it read aloud. We then made brief follow-up inquiries about how easy it was to refer back to the material and any difficulties in using it. We chose spoken explanations and follow-up conversations to reduce the burden of writing and connect understanding at the event with use at home. These conversations showed that older adults understood health information in different ways. Some could describe daily prevention practices directly, some needed to look at the material to recall them, and others found it easier to express their thoughts after someone read it aloud. Practical difficulties raised during follow-up drew our attention to how usable the material was in everyday life. We plan to enlarge key text, leave space for the contact details of someone who can help, and use specific everyday situations in further conversations. The material can help older adults find familiar reminders again and give family members a starting point for continued discussion. Future feedback will therefore consider whether the information is understandable, whether it can be found again, and where further help is needed in everyday care, allowing these needs to guide revisions.

H07Care in action

Eldercare staff: essential protective-practice training

April 10, Zhiai Eldercare Medical Facility

Design

Translate key elements—such as hand hygiene, ventilation, cleaning, and documentation—into actionable care practices.

Build & Test

Eldercare staff help maintain the everyday health environment of older adults. We used concise slides, care scenarios, demonstrations, and a commitment form to translate hand hygiene, ventilation, cleaning, and record-keeping into protective practices that could be discussed within daily work routines.

Feedback & Learn

We asked care staff to follow task cards to demonstrate procedures such as hand hygiene, then record what they had done, when they had finished, and any follow-up tasks. Peers checked whether they could use these notes to continue the work, and the group discussed where interruptions were likely. We chose practical demonstrations and handover exercises because care requires both correct actions and the transfer of key information to the next shift. Care scenarios and handover practice made gaps in the training clearer. Participants were familiar with hand hygiene procedures but still omitted times or follow-up tasks from handover notes. Staff identified interruptions during handovers as a difficulty requiring attention, helping us understand the gap between knowing what to do and ensuring continuity of work. In future training, we plan to set aside time for documentation practice, bringing completed procedures, completion times, and points for the next shift onto one prompt card, and practicing how to resume after an interruption. We hope these changes will help participants leave information that colleagues can understand more easily. The feedback also reminded us that training needs to reflect collaboration between shifts if it is to address the specific problems care staff encounter in daily work.

Part II · Biotech Explorers

Making curiosity an entry point to synthetic biology

Biotech Explorers includes learners with different ages, backgrounds, and reasons for participating. We did not position them as passive recipients of knowledge. Through drawing, visual storytelling, competitions, design, career discussion, public booths, and voting interactions, they could become questioners, narrators, creators, and contributors of perspectives. These activities introduced synthetic biology and its potential relevance to respiratory health while also addressing conditions of use, limits, and public questions.

B01–B03

B04–B05

B06–B08

Connect synthetic biology, iGEM, and sustainable science-and-innovation pathways through quizzes, careers discussion, and homecoming outreach.

B09–B10

B01Curiosity in motion

Primary-school students: “The Science Code of Breathing” science-poster showcase

June 2, Primary Division of Jilin Provincial No. 2 Experimental School

Design

Enable students to understand basic concepts of respiratory protection and detection through hand-drawing, presentation and exhibition visits.

Build & Test

We organized respiratory-health education through student-created science posters and a school showcase. Students translated protective practices, pathogen-detection ideas, and their own understanding into visual work. The posters then became starting points for peer discussion.

Feedback & Learn

Students visiting the exhibition wrote the theme of each poster, the purpose of the testing device, and anything they had not understood on comment cards. The creators pointed to their drawings as they explained their designs to peers, and the team compiled the two sets of responses separately. We combined visitor comments with the creators' explanations to compare what students intended to communicate with what readers understood and identify where more explanation was needed. Visitor comments and the creators' presentations revealed similar patterns: students found images of everyday prevention easier to understand, while explanations of the testing devices' purposes required more help. Some visitors could describe what they saw but were unsure what the device helped people find out; creators encountered the same difficulty when explaining their work. We realized that completed posters still needed to be checked with readers, allowing their creators to hear how others interpreted them. For the next exhibition, we plan to add brief explanations of purpose to posters about testing and invite students who were not involved in making them to read them. Explaining their work and responding to questions can also help students revisit their own understanding, making drawing, storytelling, and peer feedback part of the learning process.

B02Curiosity in motion

Primary-school students: “Respiration Lab” online class

June 2, Primary Division of Jilin Provincial No. 2 Experimental School

Design

Introduce basic concepts of respiratory health, biosensing and synthetic biology via story-based recorded courses.

Build & Test

This online class used “the microscopic battlefield of breathing” to connect pathogens, biosensors, and scientific exploration in a form appropriate for primary-school students. Everyday analogies, animation, open questions, and creative follow-up tasks lowered the barrier created by abstract concepts in an online setting.

Feedback & Learn

Our questionnaire after the class included questions asking students to judge conceptual statements, assess their own understanding of the mechanisms, and select material they wanted to watch again, alongside space for their questions. We considered these responses together to identify barriers to understanding in the lesson. We chose a questionnaire because an online class makes it difficult to observe every student individually, and quieter students also need time to organize and express their thoughts. Responses showed that remembering the animation did not always mean students could explain the mechanism. Some remembered how the signal changed and wanted to replay the relevant clips but still struggled to explain the connection between amplification and detection in their own words. This feedback helped us identify where the pace of the lesson needed adjustment. In future explanations, we plan to cover amplification and signal readout separately, pause the animation for students to describe what has just changed, and use everyday examples to explain further. Replay material will also be divided into short clips by concept, allowing students to return to them with specific questions. We hope to retain the curiosity prompted by the animation while giving students more time to put their thoughts into words, so that their responses can inform the pace of online teaching.

To bridge modern synthetic biology with basic respiratory protection through local narratives of dedicated healthcare professionals, thereby integrating cultural understanding, scientific literacy, and health education.

Local stories about medical care offered a familiar starting point for engaging with science, while children's retelling helped us identify where the connections between the stories, the knowledge, and everyday actions needed to be made clearer.

B04Curiosity in motion

Middle-school students: “Life and Health Through the Lens”

June 2, Middle Division of Jilin Provincial No. 2 Experimental School

Design

Enable students to connect natural life, daily protection and synthetic biology through image creation.

Build & Test

“Life and Health Through the Lens” invited middle-school students to use photography and captions to express their observations of natural life, everyday protection, and technological development. Through framing, photography, and narration, students considered what deserves to be seen and how feelings about health and life can become communicable work.

Feedback & Learn

We asked groups to exchange their photographs and captions. Peers wrote down the theme they understood, the details in an image or sentence that supported their interpretation, and anything that remained unclear. The creators revised their work in response, and the team retained the original and revised versions for comparison. We chose peer review because readers unfamiliar with the creative process are more likely to notice information that creators assume everyone already knows. Reviewing each other's work helped students see gaps in the information accompanying their images. Readers could often identify a photograph's theme but still struggled to tell which phenomenon it was meant to explain when the caption was too general. After receiving peer comments, some groups added specific scientific explanations, making the relationship between image and text clearer in their revisions. This process showed us that creators know what they intend to communicate, while readers need enough clues to understand it. In future activities, we plan to retain the first drafts, peer comments, and revisions for comparison and ask students to explain which comment they addressed. We will also guide reviewers to focus their suggestions on specific visual details and sentences, helping creators improve their explanations and encouraging students to consider readers' understanding when communicating science.

B05Curiosity in motion

Middle-school students: respiratory-detection invention-design workshop

June 2, Middle Division of Jilin Provincial No. 2 Experimental School

Design

Allow students to experience the design process from real-life pain points to conceptual sketches, and pay attention to the needs of diverse user groups.

Build & Test

We began with respiratory-health situations on campus and invited students to sketch detection concepts around users, use settings, and information needs. Examples included stationery-like, wearable, and digital concepts. The workshop emphasized a design process: identifying needs, proposing hypotheses, drawing ideas, and improving them through exchange.

Feedback & Learn

We asked students who had not helped create a design to point to its sketch and explain where to start, what to do next, and what the results meant. Designers initially remained silent, while observers noted where users paused or asked for help. Both sides then discussed revisions. We chose this paper-based trial so students could discover missing explanations by watching how someone else read their design. Peer trials revealed problems that were easy to overlook in the sketches. Users could usually find where to start but were more likely to pause when reading results, needing designers to explain the colors and symbols. These pauses gave groups more specific guidance for revision than general comments, and some groups subsequently adjusted their designs. We saw that inviting someone unfamiliar with a design to try it could help students identify information they had assumed users already knew. In the next round, we plan to ask each group to show different possible result states, pair colors with brief text, and have a new peer check the design. These exchanges also encouraged students to consider designs from the perspective of someone reading and using them, paying attention to where information was unclear and how it needed to be explained.

April 19, Jilin University Nanling Campus

Leverage springtime outdoor exhibition opportunities to use a layered instructional approach to lower the barrier to understanding in synthetic biology and detection technologies.

Public audiences: Grassland Music Festival detection-choice voting interaction

To help the public understand the appropriate use cases for different testing methods through voting and comparative demonstrations, while gathering actual user preferences.

B14Curiosity in motion

Public audiences: Gene Wars game-based synthetic-biology outreach initiative

In development

Design

Use a boundary-based game analogy to introduce concepts such as CRISPR, RNA interference, drug resistance, biofilms, and immune evasion.

Build & Test

Gene Wars is a non-commercial student outreach initiative, co-conceived by JLU-SPH iGEM and Tsinghua-A iGEM, that uses strategy-game language to introduce concepts such as CRISPR, RNA interference, antimicrobial resistance, and biofilms. It is inspired by the strategic logic of classic tower-defense games, not intended as a commercial derivative product.

Feedback & Learn

Gene Wars is still in development, and we completed a review of its content using game mechanic cards and tabletop scenarios. After reading the cards, students restated the rules, made a choice in a scenario, and explained the relevant scientific concepts, identifying which game effects could not be directly mapped onto real biological processes. Observers recorded difficulties in understanding and questions. We chose this approach to check the rules and scientific explanations before the full game launches, allowing early revisions. Students found it relatively easy to retell the rules but still confused game mechanics with scientific facts. We added side-by-side explanations of the two to the cards, and their explanations became clearer when we asked again. This led us to place the limits of the analogies where players encounter the rules, helping them understand the science as they learn how to play. We plan to add counterexamples addressing likely points of confusion and invite students unfamiliar with the original cards to read them, continuing to check whether the explanations are clear enough. We will retain the issues, changes, and follow-up checks from each round so that feedback continues to inform content development.

Feedback

Leaving a trace of real feedback

Education is not the final stage of project communication. It is one route through which a project learns from society. Privacy concerns in hospital waiting rooms, communication needs in care institutions, children’s scientific imagination, student attention to user needs, public trade-offs in voting activities, and differing requirements for pace and environment all remind us that a health-related synthetic-biology project must ask whether a technology is understandable, accessible, and worthy of trust—and who has the opportunity to participate in shaping its future.

This reflection is central to HEART-Edu Loop. We will continue to document perspectives from different communities and bring them into the revision of educational materials, project communication, and Human Practices discussion. Science does not become public knowledge simply because it has been explained once. It must be retold, examined, and shaped across people’s questions, experiences, and values.

Let an activity become someone else’s starting point

The materials developed across HEART-Edu Loop include health-communication resources for parents of infants and expectant mothers; training materials for care settings; children’s stories, movement activities, and craft materials; visual-storytelling and design tools for middle-school students; quiz, career, and homecoming-outreach frameworks for high-school students; interactive and voting formats for university and public settings; and low-stimulation, supported activity flows for diverse participants.

We understand reuse not as simple duplication but as adaptation. In a new setting, each material should be reconsidered in relation to its audience, scientific accuracy, language level, accessibility, privacy protection, and the role of partner organizations. By sharing activity plans, teaching materials, interaction templates, and guidance for use, we aim to make these experiences useful starting points for future iGEM teams, teachers, community organizations, and young science communicators.

09

From individual activities

To a continuing educational system

HEART-Edu Loop does not treat education as the delivery of correct answers from a laboratory to the public. Our work begins with real questions in different settings and continues to change through questions, participant-created work, concerns, and suggestions. For us, the value of education is not only that more people hear about synthetic biology. It is that more people have the opportunity to understand it, question it, express their expectations of it, and participate in shaping a more accessible and responsible scientific future.

From a new parent’s question to a child explaining a clay model; from a caregiver’s uncertainty in a real care scenario to students weighing different detection approaches; from a special-education teacher’s advice on activity pacing to public concerns about cost, privacy, and convenience—each form of feedback belongs to the loop. We will continue to use traceable documentation, reusable tools, and open dialogue to make education a long-term connection between science, care, and society.
